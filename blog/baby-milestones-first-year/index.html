Baby Milestones Month by Month: What to Expect

September 2026 · 8 min read

The first year of your baby's life is a whirlwind. One week they can barely hold their head up, and a few months later they're pulling themselves to standing and babbling your name. It happens fast — faster than you expect — and every month brings something new.

This guide walks through the major milestones month by month, from those hazy newborn days to the threshold of toddlerhood. You'll find developmental markers to watch for, photo ideas to capture each stage, and practical tips for keeping these memories intact. If you're also building a first-year photo book, this timeline can double as your chapter outline.

One important note before we begin: Every baby develops at their own pace. The milestones listed here are general ranges, not deadlines. Some babies roll at three months; others wait until five. Some walk at nine months; others are content to cruise until fifteen. If your baby hasn't checked a particular box by the month we mention it, that's usually perfectly normal. Trust your instincts, and talk to your pediatrician if you have specific concerns.

Month 1: The Newborn Fog

Development

Your newborn can see about 8 to 12 inches from their face — just far enough to study you while feeding. They'll startle at loud sounds, grasp your finger reflexively, and move their head from side to side during tummy time. Most of their communication happens through crying, but you'll quickly learn the difference between hungry cries and tired cries.

Photos to take

Capture the tiny details that change within weeks: wrinkly fingers, curled toes, that impossibly small nose. Photograph the hospital bracelet, the first outfit, the car seat ride home. Don't worry about lighting or composition — these raw, unpolished shots are the ones you'll treasure most. For ideas on organizing these early photos, see our guide to building a first-year photo book.

Month 2: First Real Smiles

Development

Around six to eight weeks, your baby will give you their first social smile — not gas, not a reflex, but a genuine response to your face. It changes everything. They'll start cooing, making soft vowel sounds, and tracking objects with their eyes. Head control improves during tummy time, though they'll still be wobbly.

Photos to take

Get that first smile on camera, even if it takes fifty attempts. A close-up of your baby looking at you, mid-coo, is worth more than any posed studio shot. This is also a good month to start monthly milestone photos if you haven't already — same blanket, same angle, every month for a year.

Month 3: Finding Their Hands

Development

By three months, your baby is discovering their hands. They'll stare at them, bring them together, and start batting at toys hanging above them. Neck strength improves significantly — they can hold their head steady when supported upright. You might hear the first laughs this month, and they'll start to recognize familiar faces from across the room.

Memory-keeping tip

Start a simple milestone journal alongside your photos. Write one or two sentences each week — what they did for the first time, what made them laugh, what song you sang at bedtime. These notes add context that photos alone can't capture, and they make wonderful captions when you eventually create your photo book.

Months 4–5: The Grabbing Phase

Development

Everything goes in the mouth now. Your baby can reach for and grasp objects deliberately, transfer toys from one hand to the other, and roll from tummy to back (and sometimes back to tummy). They'll respond to their name, squeal with delight, and blow bubbles with their spit. Some babies start showing interest in solid food by watching you eat intently.

Social milestones

This is when personality starts to emerge. You'll notice preferences — a favorite toy, a favorite song, a favorite person. Stranger anxiety hasn't kicked in yet, so most babies are happy being held by anyone. Enjoy this sociable phase.

Photos to take

Capture them reaching for something — a toy, your face, the dog. Photograph the look of concentration when they're trying to grab their toes. If you're using milestone cards, months four and five usually give you the most animated reactions to the props.

Milestone card ideas that actually work

  • Keep it simple — A small printed card with the month number, held next to (not on top of) your baby. Fancy letterpress cards photograph better than plastic ones.
  • Use a consistent backdrop — Same blanket or sheepskin every month. The consistency makes the growth visible when you compare photos side by side.
  • Add a size reference — Place the same stuffed animal next to your baby each month. By month twelve, the contrast is remarkable.
  • Include the outtakes — The photo where they're crying or eating the card is often better than the "perfect" one. Real beats polished.
  • Write on the back — Jot down their weight, a new skill, and one thing that made you laugh that month. Future you will be grateful.

Month 6: Sitting Up and Starting Solids

Development

Six months is a big turning point. Most babies can sit with support (and many without), giving them an entirely new perspective on the world. This is typically when you'll introduce solid food — purees or baby-led weaning, depending on your approach. Your baby will babble with consonant sounds ("ba," "da," "ma"), and they understand more than you think. They know the word "no," even if they choose to ignore it.

Photos to take

The first-food photos are universally wonderful — avocado smeared across their face, rice cereal in their eyebrows, the bewildered expression when they taste something sour. Photograph the mess. These are the pages in your photo book that everyone will laugh at.

Months 7–8: On the Move

Development

Mobility begins. Some babies army-crawl, some scoot on their bottoms, some skip straight to hands-and-knees crawling. However they do it, they're getting from point A to point B, and your house will never be the same. Fine motor skills sharpen — they can pick up small objects between thumb and forefinger (the pincer grasp) and bang two objects together with obvious delight.

Cognitive milestones

Object permanence develops around this age. Your baby understands that something still exists even when they can't see it — which is why peek-a-boo becomes hilarious and why they cry when you leave the room. Separation anxiety often peaks between seven and nine months. It's tough, but it's a sign of healthy attachment.

Photos to take

Catch them mid-crawl, headed somewhere they shouldn't be. Photograph the baby-proofing setup — the gate across the stairs, the cushion taped to the coffee table corner. Document the chaos of this stage; it's exhausting to live through but wonderful to look back on. Months 9–10: Communication Takes Off

Development

Your baby is pulling to stand, cruising along furniture, and possibly taking a few wobbly independent steps. But the biggest changes are happening in communication. They'll point at things they want, wave goodbye, clap their hands, and say a few recognizable words — "mama," "dada," "uh-oh." They understand simple instructions like "give it to me" and "where's the ball?"

Social milestones

Imitation is in full swing. They'll try to brush their own hair, hold a phone to their ear, and "read" books by turning pages (usually several at a time). They're watching everything you do and filing it away. They may show affection by leaning in for a hug or offering you their cracker.

Memory-keeping tip

Record a short video every week or two — not a staged performance, just a few seconds of ordinary life. The babbling, the crawling, the way they say "dog" as "dah." Photos are essential, but the sounds and movements of this age are something you'll want to hear again. Our new mom survival guide has more ideas for staying on top of memory-keeping without it becoming another chore.

Months 11–12: Almost a Toddler

Development

The final stretch. By their first birthday, most babies can stand independently, and many are taking their first unassisted steps. Their vocabulary might include three to five words, though they understand far more than they can say. They can follow simple directions, stack a few blocks, and use objects correctly — a spoon for eating, a brush for hair, a phone for talking (or at least pretending to).

Cognitive milestones

Problem-solving skills are growing fast. They'll figure out how to get a toy that's just out of reach, open containers to find hidden objects, and test cause-and-effect by dropping things from their high chair repeatedly (to your great delight). They're beginning to understand categories — that all dogs are "dogs," whether it's a chihuahua or a Great Dane.

Photos to take

The first birthday party is the obvious one, but don't stop there. Photograph them walking — those wide-legged, arms-up, Frankenstein steps that only last a few weeks. Capture them "helping" around the house, carrying a tiny bag, flipping through a book. Take a final monthly milestone photo and compare it to month one. The side-by-side will make you cry.

When to Talk to Your Pediatrician

Most variations in development are completely normal. But there are a few things worth mentioning at your next well-child visit:

  • No social smiles by 3 months
  • Not reaching for or grasping objects by 5 months
  • Not babbling by 7 months
  • Not sitting independently by 9 months
  • No response to their name by 9 months
  • Not pulling to stand by 12 months
  • No words or word-like sounds by 12 months
  • Loss of any previously acquired skill at any age

Early intervention makes a real difference. If something feels off, don't wait for the next scheduled appointment — call your doctor. You know your baby better than anyone, and your observations matter.

Preserving the First Year

Here's what parents tell us they wish they had done differently: taken fewer photos and organized them sooner. You don't need 500 photos per month. You need 20 to 30 good ones — the ones that tell the story of who your baby was at that moment and what your life looked like around them.

Practical tips for first-year memory keeping

  • Cull monthly — At the end of each month, spend 20 minutes selecting your best 20 to 30 photos. Delete the duplicates. Move the keepers to a dedicated album.
  • Write captions while you remember — "Tuesday morning, she rolled over for the first time and looked completely shocked" is the kind of detail you'll forget by next week.
  • Include yourself — Hand the camera to your partner, take selfies, set a timer. Your child deserves to see you in this story.
  • Don't wait until the year is over — Start your photo book at month six. Add pages as you go. A half-finished book is infinitely better than an album you never started.
  • Save the imperfect shots — Blurry action, messy backgrounds, mid-cry faces. These are the photos that feel most real five years from now.

The first year doesn't slow down because you're paying attention, but paying attention means you'll have something to hold onto when it's over. Whether you keep a written journal, a box of milestone cards, or a monthly photo book, the format matters less than the act of stopping — even briefly — to notice what's happening.

Your baby will only be this small once. The wrinkly newborn and the almost-toddler are the same person, separated by just twelve months. Documenting those months isn't about perfection or Pinterest-worthy setups. It's about making sure you remember. Because you think you will, but the details slip away faster than you'd believe.
