Baby's First Year: The Complete Photo & Memory Guide

September 2026 · 18 min read

You will take more photos in your baby's first year than in any other 12-month period of your life. Thousands of them. You'll photograph first baths and first foods and first steps and a hundred tiny moments in between. And then, almost certainly, those photos will sit on your phone — unsorted, unprinted, gradually pushed down by newer photos until you have to scroll for 45 seconds just to find them.

This guide is about doing it differently. Not about taking more photos (you'll take plenty), but about being intentional with what you capture, preserving the details you'll forget, and actually turning all those images into something your family will look at for decades.

Whether you're currently pregnant, just home from the hospital, or realizing at month eight that you should probably get organized — it's not too late. Start wherever you are.

The Hospital and First Days

The first 48 hours are a blur. You're exhausted, overwhelmed, and running on adrenaline and vending machine coffee. Photography is probably the last thing on your mind. But these are also some of the most precious — and most fleeting — moments you'll ever experience.

Here's a realistic checklist. You won't get all of these, and that's fine. But having the list means you won't realize three months later that you never got a single photo of something important.

What to photograph at the hospital

  • Baby's face in the first hour. That scrunched, puffy, bewildered expression will change within days. Even if it doesn't look like a "good" photo, you'll treasure it.
  • The size comparison. Baby in your arms, baby in the partner's arms, baby next to your hand, baby in the hospital bassinet. Newborns are impossibly tiny, and you'll forget how small they were shockingly fast.
  • The hospital details. The room, the bassinet, the whiteboard with baby's stats, the hospital bracelet. These mundane details become incredibly evocative later.
  • Skin-to-skin contact. Some of the most powerful first-year photos are the simple ones: baby on a parent's bare chest, eyes closed, completely at peace.
  • Siblings or grandparents meeting baby. The expression on a toddler's face when they first see their new sibling is irreplaceable.
  • The drive home. Baby in the car seat, impossibly small. The car seat straps dwarfing their tiny body. Pulling into the driveway. Walking through the front door.

Pro tip: Assign the photography role. If you're the one giving birth, you won't be taking photos. Make sure your partner, a family member, or a friend knows the list above. If you're planning a pregnancy photo book, these hospital photos are the perfect final chapter.

The first week at home

You'll be tempted to clean the house before taking photos. Don't. The messy kitchen counter, the pile of diapers, the laundry basket overflowing with tiny onesies — these are the real details of bringing a baby home. They tell a truer story than a staged nursery shot.

In the first week, try to capture: baby sleeping (you'll have plenty of opportunities), baby's tiny hands and feet, the nursery or sleep setup, a candid of each parent holding the baby, and at least one wide shot that shows the context — the couch you'll live on for the next month, the pile of receiving blankets, the Netflix show paused in the background.

Month-by-Month: What to Capture

Every baby develops differently. These are general milestones, not a checklist your baby needs to hit on schedule. The value here is as a photography prompt — a reminder to look for these moments so you're ready when they happen.

Month 1: The Newborn

Everything is new. Baby sleeps 16-17 hours a day, eats every 2-3 hours, and the world is a fuzzy blur to them. Capture: the curl of their fingers around yours, the way they tuck their legs up into the fetal position, their startle reflex, yawns, and the milk-drunk face after feeding. These details vanish within weeks. Also photograph the exhaustion — the 3am feeding, the parent falling asleep on the couch with baby on their chest. It's real and it's beautiful.

Month 2: First Smiles

The first real smile (not gas) changes everything. It usually arrives around 6-8 weeks and it will make every sleepless night feel worthwhile. Be ready with your phone because the first one happens fast and you might not believe it actually happened. By the end of month two, baby is starting to track objects with their eyes and making cooing sounds. Record a video of the coos — you won't remember the exact sound later.

Month 3: Discovering Hands

Baby discovers their hands exist and it's the most fascinating thing that's ever happened to them. They'll stare at their fingers, bring them to their mouth, bat at toys. They're also holding their head up during tummy time and starting to laugh. The first real laugh is a top-five parenting moment. Capture it on video if you can.

Month 4: Rolling Over

The first roll — usually tummy to back — happens around now. It's the first time baby moves themselves through space intentionally. They're grabbing toys, recognizing familiar faces, and developing a personality you can see. This is when "who does the baby look like?" conversations get serious.

Month 5-6: Sitting and Solid Foods

Baby starts sitting with support, then without. They're reaching for everything, putting everything in their mouth, and developing opinions about the world. Month six often brings the introduction of solid foods, which produces some of the most photogenic (and hilarious) moments of the entire year. Avocado-smeared faces, confused expressions at new tastes, the determination to get the spoon into their own mouth. Document all of it.

Month 7-8: Crawling and Exploration

Mobility changes the game. Baby starts crawling (or scooting, or army-crawling, or rolling to their destination). The house gets baby-proofed. Suddenly, photography becomes action photography — your subject won't stay still. Capture the determination on their face as they crawl toward something they want. Capture the chaos of a baby-proofed living room. Capture the first time they pull themselves up on furniture and look absolutely shocked at their own accomplishment.

Month 9-10: Personality Explosion

By now, baby has clear preferences, reactions, and expressions. They wave bye-bye, clap, point at things, and might say "mama" or "dada" (or something close). They're cruising along furniture, eating a wider range of foods, and showing you exactly who they are. This is when the funny photos start: the suspicious look at a new food, the triumphant grin after standing alone for two seconds, the intense concentration while playing with a cardboard box.

Month 11-12: First Steps and First Words

The finale. First steps might come now, or they might come at 14 months — both are perfectly normal. First words solidify. The first birthday party happens. Capture the wobbly steps, the cake smash, the overwhelmed expression at a roomful of people singing. And take one more quiet photo: your baby, who is now almost a toddler, asleep in your arms. Because this is the last time they'll be this small.

Monthly Milestone Photos

The monthly milestone photo has become a tradition for good reason: it creates a visual timeline that makes the speed of growth viscerally clear. Seeing month 1 next to month 12 is staggering. Here's how to do it well.

Consistency is everything

The power of monthly photos comes from the comparison. Use the same setup every month:

  • Same location. A specific chair, blanket, or corner of the nursery. The consistent background makes the baby's growth the star.
  • Same time of day. After a nap is ideal — baby is alert, fed, and (relatively) cooperative.
  • Same angle. Overhead for a baby on a blanket, or straight-on for a baby in a chair. Keep it consistent so the progression is clear.
  • A size reference. A stuffed animal, a specific pillow, or a milestone card with the month number. The stuffed animal approach is especially effective because it goes from "bigger than the baby" to "dwarfed by the toddler."

Practical tips

Use natural light — near a window, in the morning or late afternoon. Avoid flash, which creates harsh shadows and startles babies. Take more photos than you think you need; babies don't hold poses. Use burst mode on your phone for the 3-second window when baby is looking at you and not crying or lunging for the milestone card. And don't stress if the month-3 photo has baby crying or the month-7 photo shows them crawling away. Those "imperfect" shots often become the favorites.

If you're using an app to add text or stickers to milestone photos, do it right away. If you wait, you'll have 300 unlabeled photos and no idea which month is which.

Beyond Milestones: The Moments You'll Actually Miss

Milestones get all the attention because they're easy to plan for. But the moments that hit hardest when you look back are almost always the ordinary ones. These are the things no milestone chart tells you to photograph:

  • Tiny details that change. Photograph their hands at one month — the paper-thin fingernails, the way they grip your finger. Their feet. Their eyelashes (impossibly long by month three). The soft spot on their head. The peach fuzz on their ears. These details disappear and you will not remember them.
  • The feeding routine. Whether it's breastfeeding, bottle feeding, or both, this is where you spend an enormous amount of time in the first year. One good photo of a 2am feeding — the dim light, the tired parent, the peaceful baby — tells the story better than any milestone shot.
  • Bath time. The slippery joy of a baby in a tiny tub. The hooded towel afterward. The post-bath sleepy calm.
  • The everyday backdrop. The living room with the play mat and bouncer and toys scattered everywhere. The changing table setup. The stroller walks. Your house will look like this for only a short time, and the mess is part of the story.
  • Funny faces and weird moments. The suspicious squint at a new food. The intense focus while trying to pick up a cheerio. The scream-laugh while being tickled. The startled expression when the dog barks. These are personality.
  • The parents. This is the biggest blind spot. The primary caregiver takes thousands of photos and appears in almost none of them. Make a conscious effort to hand the phone to your partner, to set a timer, or to ask visitors to take photos of you with the baby. Our guide to documenting childhood goes deeper into this.

Including Non-Photo Memories

A baby's first year photo book doesn't have to be only photos. Some of the most meaningful pages are the ones that capture things a camera can't.

Physical keepsakes to photograph or scan

  • Hospital bracelet. Photograph it before it gets lost in a drawer. Include the date, time, and weight if visible.
  • Footprint and handprint. Most hospitals do one at birth. Do another at 6 months and 12 months for a stunning comparison.
  • First outfit. Lay it flat, photograph it. Then photograph the same outfit at 12 months (it won't fit past their knee).
  • Growth chart. A photo of the pencil marks on the wall, or a written record of weight and length at each checkup.
  • Cards and notes. The congratulations cards, the notes from grandparents, the "welcome baby" banner from the front door.

Written memories

Things you think you'll remember but won't:

  • What time baby typically woke up and went to sleep
  • Their favorite song or lullaby at each stage
  • The specific sound of their first laugh
  • What they called things before they knew the real words
  • The funny thing they did every single day that drove you crazy and that you now can't recall
  • The book they wanted read over and over and over
  • What you were watching on TV during those late-night feedings

Write these down. A note on your phone, a running document, a sentence a day. When you make the photo book, these details become captions that transform a nice photo into a time machine. If you're making a photo book as a gift for a new mom, ask family members to contribute their own memories and observations.

Gear and Photography Tips

Phone vs. camera

Short answer: use your phone. The best camera is the one that's in your hand when the moment happens, and your phone is always in your hand. Modern smartphone cameras are genuinely excellent for the kind of photos that make great baby books — close-ups, natural light, candid moments.

A dedicated camera is worth it only if you already own one and are comfortable using it. The learning curve of a DSLR or mirrorless camera, combined with the chaos of a newborn, means most people end up missing moments while fiddling with settings. If you do use a dedicated camera, shoot in aperture priority mode (f/2.8 or lower for that soft background blur) and auto-ISO.

For detailed phone photography techniques, see our iPhone photo tips for parents guide.

Lighting

Natural light is your best friend. Place baby near a window — not in direct sunlight, but in the soft, indirect light that comes from a north-facing window or a window with sheer curtains. This light is flattering, consistent, and makes skin tones look natural.

Avoid overhead room lights, which create shadows under the eyes. Avoid flash, which is harsh, flattens everything, and can startle babies. For nighttime moments (and there will be many), embrace the low light. A slightly grainy 3am photo lit by a single lamp is more honest and more beautiful than a flash-blasted one.

Shooting techniques

  • Get on their level. Photographs taken from baby's eye level are more intimate and engaging than photos shot from standing height looking down.
  • Use burst mode. Babies are unpredictable. Take 20 photos and pick the best one. Your phone can handle it.
  • Use portrait mode strategically. The background blur (bokeh) of portrait mode is beautiful for close-ups, but it can clip ears, fingers, and wisps of hair. Check the edges.
  • Capture the context. Not every photo needs to be a close-up of baby's face. Pull back and show the room, the scene, the chaos. Wide shots tell the story. Close-ups capture the emotion.
  • Include your hands. Your hands holding baby's feet. Your partner's hand on baby's back during tummy time. Hands provide scale and connection without needing to stage a portrait.

The Partner and Grandparent Problem

One parent usually becomes the family photographer. This means two things: that parent is in almost no photos, and the other parent has almost no photos on their phone. Both are problems.

Getting in the photos yourself

  • Hand off the phone. Literally give your phone to your partner and say "take a few photos of me with the baby." It feels awkward. Do it anyway. Your child will want to see you.
  • Use the timer. Set your phone on a shelf or stack of books, set the 10-second timer, and get in the shot. You don't need a tripod.
  • Ask visitors. When grandparents, friends, or relatives visit, ask them to take a few candid shots of you. People are happy to do this if you ask.
  • Accept imperfect photos of yourself. You're exhausted. Your hair is a mess. You're wearing the same shirt for the third day. None of that will matter in 10 years. What will matter is that your child can see you were there.

Getting photos from others

Grandparents take photos too. So do aunts, uncles, friends, and babysitters. Those photos are valuable because they show your baby from a different perspective — and they often capture moments you missed because you were in them.

  • Create a shared album. Google Photos shared albums or Apple Shared Photo Library make this effortless. Share the album link with everyone who spends time with baby and ask them to add their photos.
  • Ask specifically. "Can you send me any photos you took today?" works better than hoping people will share spontaneously.
  • Get the video calls. If long-distance grandparents FaceTime regularly, screenshot those calls occasionally. The split-screen of a baby staring at a grandparent's face on the screen is surprisingly emotional in a photo book. What Parents Wish They'd Done Differently

We've talked to hundreds of parents who've made first-year photo books. These are the regrets that come up again and again:

"I have 3,000 photos of the baby and maybe 5 of me holding the baby. My daughter will never know what I looked like during her first year."
"I wish I'd taken more video. Photos are great but they don't capture the sound of his laugh or the way he said 'mamamama' at everything."
"I was so focused on getting the perfect monthly milestone photo that I forgot to just take normal, everyday photos. Now my book looks like a posed portrait series instead of a story about our life."
"I wish I'd written things down. I can see in the photos that she's eating solid food for the first time, but I can't remember what it was or how she reacted."
"We waited until she was two to make the book. By then we'd changed phones, some photos were lost, and I couldn't remember the order of anything. Start sooner."
"I didn't photograph the mess. The house, the laundry, the chaos. I staged everything. Now it doesn't look like our life. It looks like a stock photo."

The pattern is clear: parents regret the things they didn't capture, not the imperfect photos they did. A blurry photo of a real moment is worth more than no photo at all.

Building the Book

You've taken the photos. You've (hopefully) written some notes. Now what? Here's how to turn a year's worth of memories into a photo book you'll actually want on your shelf.

Chronological vs. thematic

Chronological is the most natural structure and what most parents choose. It follows the arc of the year: hospital, newborn, first smiles, first foods, first steps, first birthday. The advantage is that it mirrors how you experienced the year and makes the growth progression visible.

Thematic organizes by topic: a section on bath time photos, a section on grandparent visits, a section on funny faces, a section on milestones. This can work well if you have a lot of photos in specific categories, but it loses the sense of time passing.

Our recommendation: Chronological with thematic touches. Follow the timeline, but allow yourself to create a spread of "all the bath time faces" or "foods you loved and hated" within the chronological flow. This gives you both the narrative arc and the fun collections. For layout ideas, see our photo book layout tips.

How many pages

For a first-year book, 60-100 pages is the sweet spot. That's roughly 5-8 pages per month, which gives you enough room for both milestone moments and everyday details without the book becoming so long that you never look through it. If you have a lot of photos, resist the urge to include everything. Curation is what makes a great book. Choose the 150-200 best photos from your thousands, and save the rest digitally. For help organizing, read our phone photo organization guide or our family photo organization guide.

What text to add

Less than you think, but more than nothing. Here's what works:

  • Chapter headings by month. "Month Three: You discovered your hands today and spent 45 minutes staring at them." Personal and specific beats generic.
  • Captions on key photos. Not every photo needs one. But the ones that tell a story your child can't see — who that person is, what was happening, why everyone's laughing — are invaluable.
  • A letter. Many parents include a letter to their baby at the beginning or end of the book. Write it now, while the feelings are fresh. You don't need to be a writer. Just be honest. "This year was the hardest and best year of our lives. Here's what happened." Our letter to my child guide can help.
  • Stats and data. Birth weight and length. Weight at 6 months. Weight at 12 months. First word and when they said it. First food. First tooth. These facts feel mundane now but become fascinating to your child later.

When to Start (The Answer Is Now)

The best time to start organizing your baby's first year photos was the day they were born. The second best time is right now.

Here's the practical reality: the longer you wait, the harder it gets. Not because the photos disappear (though that does happen — phone upgrades, cloud storage changes, accidentally deleted albums), but because your memory of what happened fades. At month three, you remember the details of month one clearly. At month twelve, month one is already hazy. At age three, the entire first year is a warm blur.

If you're currently pregnant, set up the system now. Create a shared album. Save this guide. Write down your due date and set a reminder for one month after to start your monthly milestone photo series.

If baby is already here and you haven't started — that's fine. Most parents are in the same position. The photos are on your phone. The memories are in your head. You just need to get them out before they fade.

A few quick-start options:

  • 15-minute version: Create a "Favorites" album in your phone's photo app. Spend 15 minutes scrolling through the year and adding your best 100-150 photos. That's enough for a book.
  • 1-hour version: Do the favorites selection, then organize them by month. Write one sentence per month about what was happening. You now have a book outline.
  • Done-for-you version: Share your entire photo library or Google Photos album with a service like Remeet that handles the curation and design for you. You review and approve; they do the work.

Don't let perfectionism stop you. A finished photo book with "only" your 100 best photos is infinitely more valuable than a hypothetical perfect book with all 3,000 photos that you never get around to making.

Your baby's first year happens once. The photos on your phone are already a miracle — a few thousand frozen moments from the most transformative year of your life. Give them a home that's worthy of what they represent.
