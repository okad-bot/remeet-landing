Baby's First Year Photo Book: What to Include (and What to Skip)

September 2026 · 7 min read

You took 3,000 photos of your baby this year. Maybe more. Every smile, every nap, every messy face — documented on your phone. Now the year is ending and you want to turn those photos into something real. Something you can hold.

But where do you start? How do you choose from thousands of photos? And how do you make it feel like a story, not just a collection?

Here's what we've learned from designing hundreds of first-year photo books.

Think in chapters, not months

The most common mistake is organizing by month: "Month 1, Month 2, Month 3..." This creates 12 chapters that all feel the same — a few milestones, a few cute faces, repeat.

Instead, organize by story. Here are chapter ideas that actually work:

  • The day you arrived — Hospital or birth center photos, first family photo, first hours
  • Coming home — The car ride, the nursery, meeting siblings or pets, that first night
  • The early days — Newborn details (tiny hands, feet, yawns), sleep-deprived selfies, visitors
  • Finding our rhythm — Daily routines, feeding, bath time, the chaos of figuring it out
  • First discoveries — Reaching, grabbing, rolling, sitting up, tasting food
  • Our people — Grandparents, friends, family gatherings, the village that helped
  • Adventures — First trip, first beach, first flight, first snow
  • The everyday — Morning light in the nursery, walks in the stroller, toys on the floor
  • Growing up — Standing, first steps, first words, the personality emerging

Not every chapter needs to be in your book. Pick the ones that feel true to your year.

What to include

The obvious ones

First smile, first laugh, first solid food, first steps. These are the milestones everyone expects, and they should be in the book.

The less obvious ones (these make the book special)

  • The "in between" moments — Dad asleep with baby on his chest. The mess after lunch. The mountain of laundry. These photos feel mundane now but become the most treasured later.
  • Details — Close-ups of tiny fingers wrapped around yours. The stuffed animal they wouldn't let go of. Their favorite onesie.
  • The parents — You're in this story too. Include selfies, mirror shots, candid moments of you being a parent. Your child will want to see these.
  • The setting — Your apartment, the kitchen where you made all those bottles, the park you walked to every day. These places tell the story of where you lived this year.
  • Imperfect photos — Slightly blurry, bad lighting, awkward angle — but capturing something real. A photo book isn't a portfolio. It's a memory.

What to skip

  • Near-duplicates — You don't need 15 versions of the same smile. Pick the best 2-3.
  • Screenshots — Text conversations, app screenshots, video thumbnails. These don't print well and break the visual flow.
  • Photos that need context to understand — If you can't tell what's happening without a 3-sentence explanation, it probably won't work in a book.
  • Professional photo session outtakes — Keep the best 3-4 from each session. The rest feel repetitive in a book about everyday life.

How many photos is enough?

For a 60-80 page book, you'll typically use 80-150 photos. That means curating from thousands down to about a hundred.

This is where most people get stuck. Choosing 100 photos from 3,000 is hard. It feels like leaving important moments out.

Two approaches:

  1. Curate yourself: Set aside an afternoon. Go through your camera roll month by month, favoriting the photos that make you feel something. Don't overthink it — your gut reaction is usually right. Aim for 15-20 favorites per month.
  2. Send everything: Share your entire camera roll or Google Photos library with a service that curates for you. They'll find the strongest moments, remove duplicates, and build a story. This is what Remeet does — you send 3,000 photos, we select the best 100-150.

Captions that work

You don't need to caption every photo. But the right caption turns a nice photo into a memory with weight.

Good captions are:

  • Specific: "Your first time trying avocado — you were not impressed" beats "6 months old"
  • Emotional: "I remember this exact feeling" or "This was the hardest and best week"
  • Short: One sentence is enough. Two max.
  • Written to your child: "You" and "your" make captions personal — this book is for them, eventually
The best caption we've seen: "You slept on him for the first time. He didn't move for two hours."

The biggest mistake: waiting too long

Here's the truth: most first-year photo books never get made. Not because parents don't want them, but because the project feels too big and there's never a good time.

A year passes. Then two. The details fade. You can't remember if that photo was from month 4 or month 6. The captions that would have been vivid become generic.

Don't wait for the perfect moment to start. Don't wait until you have time to design it yourself. The best first-year book is the one that exists.
