Photo Book Paper Types: Matte vs Glossy vs Lustre

September 2026 · 8 min read

You've selected your photos, figured out the layout, and you're ready to print. Then the paper selection screen appears: matte, glossy, lustre, satin, silk. What do these words actually mean, and which one should you choose?

Paper is one of the most important decisions you'll make when creating a photo book. It affects how your colors look, how the pages feel in your hands, how light reflects off each image, and how long the book lasts. This guide breaks down the three main paper types so you can choose with confidence.

The three main paper types

Almost every photo book service offers some variation of these three finishes. The names vary slightly — one company's "satin" is another's "lustre" — but the characteristics are consistent.

Matte paper

Matte paper has no shine at all. The surface is flat and smooth, absorbing light rather than reflecting it. When you run your finger across matte paper, it feels soft and slightly textured, like high-end stationery.

Strengths:

  • Zero glare. You can view matte pages under any lighting — overhead lights, direct sunlight, reading lamps — without reflections obscuring the images. This makes matte the best choice for books that will be displayed on coffee tables or read under varied lighting conditions.
  • Fingerprint resistant. Matte surfaces don't show fingerprints, smudges, or oils from handling. The book stays clean-looking even after dozens of viewings. This is a significant advantage for books that children or grandparents will handle frequently.
  • Elegant, understated look. Matte has a quiet sophistication. Colors are slightly muted, which gives images an editorial, gallery-like quality. Fine art photographers and wedding albums often use matte for this reason.
  • Better for text. If your photo book includes substantial captions, journal entries, or text overlays, matte paper makes them easier to read. There's no glare competing with the letterforms.

Weaknesses:

  • Reduced color vibrancy. Because matte paper absorbs more light, colors appear slightly less saturated than on glossy paper. Bright reds, deep blues, and vivid greens lose a small degree of punch.
  • Lower contrast. Blacks on matte paper aren't as deep as on glossy. Dark areas in photos can appear slightly gray or washed out, especially in images with dramatic shadows.
  • Can feel flat. For photos that are all about vivid color — tropical sunsets, flower gardens, food photography — matte can make the images feel underpowered.

Best for: Black and white photography, portraits, wedding albums, text-heavy books, books that will be handled frequently, fine art or editorial-style layouts.

Glossy paper

Glossy paper has a high-shine, reflective surface. It looks and feels like a traditional photograph — smooth, bright, and almost wet-looking. This is the finish most people associate with "photos."

Strengths:

  • Maximum color vibrancy. Glossy paper delivers the most saturated, vivid colors of any finish. Reds pop, blues deepen, and greens glow. If your photos are all about color — a trip to Morocco, autumn foliage, a child's birthday party — glossy makes them sing.
  • Deep blacks and high contrast. Shadow areas in photos retain their depth on glossy paper. This gives images a more dynamic range, making both highlights and shadows more pronounced.
  • Sharp detail rendering. The smooth, reflective surface of glossy paper shows fine details crisply. Tiny textures, hair strands, and intricate patterns are rendered with maximum clarity.
  • Familiar feel. Glossy looks and feels like a traditional photo print. For people who grew up with photo albums full of glossy 4x6 prints, this finish feels natural and photo-like.

Weaknesses:

  • Glare and reflections. Under overhead lights or near windows, glossy pages throw back reflections that can obscure parts of the image. You end up tilting the book to find an angle where you can actually see the photo.
  • Fingerprints and smudges. Every touch leaves a mark. Glossy pages show fingerprints, oils, and smudges immediately. If the book will be passed around a family gathering, it will need wiping down afterward.
  • Pages can stick. In humid conditions or if pages are pressed together, glossy surfaces can stick to each other. Over time, this can damage the finish or even transfer ink between pages.
  • Can feel cheap at lower weights. Thin glossy paper feels flimsy and reminds people of magazine pages rather than a premium book. If you go glossy, invest in a heavier paper weight.

Best for: Travel and landscape photography, food photography, colorful events, children's photos, anyone who prioritizes color accuracy and vibrancy above all else.

Lustre (satin) paper

Lustre paper — sometimes called satin or semi-gloss — sits between matte and glossy. It has a subtle sheen that's visible but not reflective, with a very fine texture you can feel when you run your fingertip across the surface.

Strengths:

  • Best of both worlds. Lustre delivers more color vibrancy than matte while producing far less glare than glossy. It's the compromise that actually works, rather than a watered-down version of either extreme.
  • Professional standard. Lustre is the most commonly used finish among professional photographers for client prints and albums. There's a reason: it handles the widest range of photo styles well.
  • Minimal fingerprints. The subtle texture of lustre paper resists fingerprints better than glossy, though not quite as well as matte. For books that will be handled regularly, this is a meaningful advantage.
  • Good under varied lighting. Lustre pages are readable under most lighting conditions. You might see a faint sheen under direct light, but never the hard reflections that plague glossy paper.
  • Rich color with deep blacks. The slight sheen of lustre paper helps maintain good color saturation and black depth without the harshness of full glossy.

Weaknesses:

  • Neither extreme. If you specifically want the absolute maximum color punch, glossy wins. If you specifically want the most understated, gallery feel, matte wins. Lustre is excellent but it's a compromise by definition.
  • Inconsistent naming. "Lustre," "satin," "semi-gloss," and "silk" are used interchangeably across different services, and the actual finish varies. What one company calls "lustre" might look different from another's version. Check samples if possible.
  • Slightly higher cost. Some services charge a small premium for lustre paper, though the difference is usually modest.

Best for: Mixed-content books (some portraits, some landscapes, some events), books where you're unsure which finish to choose, professional-quality results for any subject matter.

Quick comparison

  • Matte: No glare, no fingerprints, muted colors, elegant feel. Best for portraits, B&W, text-heavy books.
  • Glossy: Maximum color and contrast, shows glare and fingerprints, vibrant and sharp. Best for travel, landscapes, colorful subjects.
  • Lustre: Moderate sheen, good color, minimal glare, professional standard. Best for mixed-content books and when you can't decide.

Paper weight and thickness

Beyond the finish, paper weight significantly affects how your book feels. Paper weight is measured in GSM (grams per square meter). Higher GSM means thicker, heavier pages.

  • 150-170 GSM: Standard weight. Pages feel like a thick magazine page. Fine for casual books, but can feel flimsy in a premium hardcover.
  • 200-250 GSM: Premium weight. Pages feel substantial and don't flop or bend easily. This is the sweet spot for most photo books — heavy enough to feel luxurious, light enough that a 60-page book isn't unwieldy.
  • 300+ GSM: Card stock weight. Pages feel almost rigid, like individual cards. This is typically found in board books or high-end lay-flat albums. Beautiful but adds significant weight and cost to the book.

If you're choosing between paper weight options, go heavier than you think you need. The tactile experience of turning thick, weighty pages elevates the entire book. Thin pages feel disappointing in a hardcover binding, regardless of how good the print quality is.

Lay-flat vs. regular binding

Binding type affects how pages sit when the book is open, and it's closely related to paper choice.

Regular binding (perfect bound or case bound) creates a slight curve at the center of each spread. Photos that cross the center gutter are interrupted by the curve, and a thin strip of the image is hidden in the binding. For most layouts, this is fine — you just avoid placing important subjects at the center of a two-page spread.

Lay-flat binding allows pages to open completely flat with no curve or gap at the center. This means panoramic photos and two-page spreads display seamlessly from edge to edge. Lay-flat binding typically uses thicker paper (often 300+ GSM board) because the pages need rigidity to lie flat.

Lay-flat is worth the upgrade for books that feature a lot of landscape photography, panoramic shots, or full-spread layouts. For portrait-oriented books with single images per page, regular binding works perfectly well. Learn more about binding options on our quality and materials page.

Color accuracy across paper types

If color accuracy is your primary concern — for a professional portfolio or a book of carefully edited photos — here's what to know:

  • Glossy reproduces colors closest to what you see on screen. The reflective surface renders a wider color gamut, meaning more shades are distinguishable. What you see on a calibrated monitor is closest to what you'll get on glossy paper.
  • Matte shifts colors slightly. The light-absorbing surface mutes colors by a small margin. Saturated reds might appear slightly less intense, and deep shadows might lift slightly. This isn't a flaw — it's the physics of light absorption — but it's worth knowing if exact color matching matters.
  • Lustre lands in between. Color reproduction is very close to glossy but without the reflections that make glossy hard to view consistently.

For the vast majority of personal photo books, the color differences between paper types are subtle enough that any finish will look great. The choice is more about the feel and viewing experience than color precision.

Durability and longevity

A photo book should last decades. Paper choice affects how well it ages:

  • Matte is the most durable surface. Without a reflective coating to scratch or peel, matte pages hold up well to handling, stacking, and storage. They're also the most forgiving if the book gets slightly damp — the textured surface doesn't show water spots the way glossy does.
  • Glossy is the most vulnerable to surface damage. Scratches, scuffs, and water spots are visible on reflective surfaces. Glossy pages can also stick together if stored in warm or humid conditions, potentially damaging the finish over time.
  • Lustre offers good durability. The textured surface resists scratches better than glossy, and the lower reflectivity means minor scuffs are less visible. For books that will be handled regularly over many years, lustre is a solid choice.

Regardless of paper type, store your photo books upright (not stacked) in a cool, dry place away from direct sunlight. UV light fades all prints over time, and humidity is the enemy of all paper. For more on what goes into a well-made photo book, see our photo book comparison guide.

How to choose based on your photos

If you're still unsure, use your photos as the guide:

  • Mostly portraits and people? Go matte or lustre. Skin tones look natural and flattering on both, and the reduced glare makes faces easier to appreciate.
  • Mostly landscapes and travel? Go glossy or lustre. Vivid skies, water, and greenery benefit from the color intensity that glossy and lustre provide.
  • Mostly black and white? Go matte. B&W images on matte paper have a gallery-quality feel that glossy can't match. The absence of glare lets the tonal range speak for itself.
  • A mix of everything? Go lustre. It handles the widest range of subjects competently and is the safest choice when your book includes diverse photo styles.
  • Heavy on text and captions? Go matte. Text is easiest to read on non-reflective surfaces, and the result looks intentionally designed rather than photo-first.

When in doubt, lustre is the safest bet. It's the paper type that produces the fewest regrets, which is why most professional photographers default to it for client work.
