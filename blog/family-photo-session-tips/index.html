Family Photo Session: How to Prepare & What to Wear

September 2026 · 8 min read

You booked the photographer. The date is on the calendar. And now the anxiety sets in: What do we wear? How do we get the kids to cooperate? What if it rains? What if everyone's cranky?

Deep breath. A family photo session doesn't need to be stressful. With a little preparation — the right outfits, a smart timeline, and realistic expectations about small children — you can walk away with photos you'll genuinely love.

Here's everything you need to know.

What to wear: the outfit guide

Clothing is the number one source of pre-session stress. It doesn't have to be. The goal isn't matching — it's coordinating.

The golden rule: coordinate, don't match

Matching outfits (everyone in white shirts and jeans) looked great in 2005. Today, it reads as costume-y. Instead, pick a color palette of 3-4 complementary colors and let each family member express their own style within it.

For example:

  • Warm palette: Rust, cream, olive, mustard. Mom in a rust dress, Dad in olive chinos and cream sweater, kids in mustard and cream tones.
  • Cool palette: Navy, dusty blue, gray, white. Dad in navy, Mom in dusty blue, kids in gray and white.
  • Earthy palette: Tan, brown, sage, ivory. Works beautifully in outdoor settings with natural backdrops.
  • Jewel tones: Burgundy, emerald, gold, navy. Perfect for fall sessions and wooded locations.

What works

  • Solid colors or subtle patterns. Small prints (fine stripes, delicate florals) are fine. Large logos, bold graphics, and busy plaids compete with faces.
  • Layers and texture. A cardigan, a scarf, rolled-up sleeves, a denim jacket — these add visual interest and dimension to photos.
  • Fitted but comfortable. Clothes that fit well photograph better than oversized items. But if your kid only cooperates in their favorite soft pants, let them wear the soft pants.
  • Shoes that make sense. You'll likely be walking on grass, gravel, or sand. Leave the stilettos at home. Boots, sandals, and clean sneakers all work.

What to avoid

  • Neon colors — they reflect onto skin and create strange color casts
  • All black — loses detail in photos, especially in shadows
  • All white — can blow out in bright light
  • Brand logos or character prints — they date the photo instantly and draw the eye away from faces
  • Brand-new outfits your kids have never worn — if it's itchy, stiff, or unfamiliar, they'll be miserable. Do a test run at home.

Start with one outfit, build from there

Pick the outfit for the hardest-to-dress person first (usually Mom or the teenager). Then coordinate everyone else around it. If Mom's wearing a sage green dress, Dad's palette is earth tones, and the kids get cream, tan, or complementary greens.

Lay everything out the night before. Take a photo of all the outfits together on the bed. You'll immediately see if something clashes.

When to schedule: timing matters

Golden hour is golden for a reason

The hour after sunrise and the hour before sunset produce the most flattering light. It's warm, soft, directional, and forgiving. No squinting, no harsh shadows under eyes, no washed-out faces.

For most families, the evening golden hour works better than sunrise (because nobody wants to get three kids dressed and out the door at 5:30 AM). Check sunset times for your session date and plan to start 60-90 minutes before sunset.

Overcast days are secretly great

Cloud cover acts like a giant softbox, diffusing light evenly. You won't get dramatic sunset shots, but you will get soft, even light that's flattering for everyone. No squinting. If your session day is overcast, don't panic — your photographer will be relieved.

Avoid midday sun

The sun directly overhead (roughly 11 AM to 2 PM) creates harsh shadows, makes everyone squint, and is the least flattering light for faces. If your photographer suggests a midday slot, find a new photographer.

Work around your kids' schedule

This is more important than perfect light. A well-rested, recently-fed toddler in slightly flat light will produce infinitely better photos than a hungry, overtired toddler in gorgeous golden hour. Schedule around nap times. If your toddler naps from 1-3, don't book a 3:30 session — they'll be groggy and miserable. Aim for a time when they're reliably at their best.

Location scouting: where to shoot

Outdoor locations

  • Parks and fields: Open grass areas with scattered trees are the most versatile. Trees provide shade options and visual framing. Look for parks with variety — a meadow, a tree-lined path, a bench.
  • Beach: Beautiful but windy. Hair will fly everywhere. Embrace it or bring hair ties. Late afternoon light on water is stunning. Barefoot is the way to go.
  • Urban settings: Brick walls, quiet streets, courtyards, murals. Great for families who want a more modern, editorial feel. Pick locations with minimal foot traffic so you're not dodging pedestrians.
  • Your own backyard: Seriously. If you have a nice outdoor space, it's comfortable, familiar to kids, and you don't have to drive anywhere. The porch, the garden, the big tree — these are part of your family's story.

Indoor locations

Lifestyle sessions at home have become increasingly popular. They capture your family in your actual space — reading on the couch, cooking together, playing on the floor. These sessions feel authentic because they are. Make sure rooms are tidy but not sterile. Real life with a bit of editing.

Seasonal considerations

  • Spring: Flowers and blossoms. Watch for allergies and muddy ground.
  • Summer: Lush greens, long golden hours. Bring bug spray and water.
  • Fall: Peak family photo season for a reason. The colors do half the work. Book early — every photographer is slammed September through November.
  • Winter: Bare trees, snow, cozy layers. Shorter daylight means shorter windows. The cold keeps kids moving, which can actually produce more natural, active shots.

Preparing kids (the hardest part)

Let's be honest: kids are the wildcard. You can control outfits, location, and timing, but you cannot control a three-year-old's mood. You can, however, set conditions for success.

Before the session

  • Talk about it positively. "We're going to go play at the park and someone is going to take pictures of us" sounds much better than "You need to sit still and smile."
  • Don't overhype it. Some kids get anxious with too much buildup. Keep it casual.
  • Feed them. A full stomach prevents meltdowns. Bring non-staining snacks (Goldfish, pretzels, apple slices). Avoid anything with chocolate, berries, or red dye.
  • Nap them. A rested child is a cooperative child. Protect the nap at all costs on photo day.
  • Bring a secret weapon. A favorite small toy, a lollipop (for between takes), bubbles, or a silly joke — anything that produces genuine smiles.

During the session

  • Let them move. Stiff, posed portraits of kids rarely look natural. Walk together, play, chase, tickle. The best kid photos happen between the poses.
  • Don't say "smile." It produces grimaces. Instead, whisper something absurd in their ear. Tell a fart joke. Race them to a tree. Genuine reactions beat forced smiles every time.
  • Take breaks. If a child is done, they're done. Take five minutes, let them run, have a snack, regroup. Pushing through rarely works.
  • Lower your expectations. You don't need every single photo to be perfect. You need 10-15 great ones. Let the photographer work. Trust the process.

Posing tips: how to look natural

The best family photos feel candid even when they're loosely directed. Here are some prompts that produce natural-looking shots:

  • Walk toward the camera. Hold hands, swing the kids, talk to each other. Walking shots have natural movement and avoid the stiff lineup look.
  • Forehead to forehead. Parents lean in, foreheads touching, eyes closed. Simple, intimate, beautiful.
  • Lift or swing the kids. Airplane, shoulder rides, swinging between parents — these produce genuine joy and dynamic compositions.
  • Whisper and laugh. Whisper something funny to your partner. The photographer catches the real laugh, not the pose.
  • Sit or lie down. Getting on the ground with kids puts everyone on the same level. Sit in a circle, lie on a blanket, pile on the couch.
  • Individual portraits. Don't forget these. Each parent with each child alone. These become treasured photos you'll wish you had more of as kids grow up. As we discuss in our guide to documenting childhood, the everyday moments matter most.

Choosing a photographer

What to look for

  • Portfolio consistency. Look at their last 20-30 sessions, not just the highlight reel. The editing style, poses, and quality should be consistent throughout.
  • Families that look like yours. If you have three kids under five, look for a photographer who regularly shoots families with young children. They'll know how to handle the chaos.
  • Editing style you love. Light and airy? Rich and moody? True to color? You can't easily change the editing style after the fact. Pick a photographer whose natural style matches your taste.
  • Full gallery examples. Any photographer can produce 5 amazing photos from a session. Ask to see a full delivered gallery. That tells you what to actually expect.

Questions to ask

  • How many final edited photos will we receive?
  • How long until we get our gallery?
  • Do you provide outfit guidance?
  • What happens if it rains? (There should be a rain date policy.)
  • Do you have experience with kids our children's age?
  • Can we see a full gallery from a recent session?

Budget expectations

Family session pricing varies widely by market. Expect $200-400 for a newer photographer, $400-800 for an established local photographer, and $1,000+ for premium or destination photographers. Most sessions last 45-90 minutes and deliver 30-75 edited images.

What to do with the photos after

This is where most families drop the ball. You invest time and money in a beautiful session, get the digital gallery, post a few on social media, and then... the photos sit in a download folder forever.

Don't let that happen. Here's a simple post-session checklist:

  1. Back them up. Download the full-resolution files. Save them to at least two places: your computer and a cloud service. These are irreplaceable.
  2. Pick your favorites. Choose 15-20 that you love. These are the ones you'll actually use.
  3. Print something. A canvas for the wall, a framed print for the grandparents, or a family yearbook that includes the session alongside your everyday snapshots. Professional photos mixed with iPhone photos from daily life create the most authentic record of your year.
  4. Update the frames. If you have family photos on display, swap in the new ones. It sounds obvious, but many families display the same photos for years.
  5. Share with family. Send the gallery link to grandparents, aunts, uncles. Or better yet, order a photo book for the grandparents and include a mix of session photos and candid everyday moments.

Your professional photos will look stunning in a photo book alongside the everyday shots you take with your phone. It's the combination of polished portraits and messy real life that tells the full story of your family. The most important tip

Here's what experienced family photographers will tell you: the sessions that produce the best photos aren't the ones with perfect outfits and flawless behavior. They're the ones where the family is relaxed, connected, and actually enjoying each other's company.

If your toddler throws a tantrum, that's part of the story. If your teenager rolls their eyes, that's authentic. If the dog photobombs every shot, that's hilarious and you'll love those photos in ten years.

Prepare what you can control. Let go of what you can't. And focus on being together — the photographer will capture the rest.
