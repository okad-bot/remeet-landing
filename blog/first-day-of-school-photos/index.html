First Day of School Photos: Ideas + How to Make a Tradition Book

September 2026 · 6 min read

Every August or September, the same ritual plays out in millions of households: the new backpack, the outfit laid out the night before, the sign with their grade and teacher's name, the front-door photo. It takes three minutes and it's one of the most powerful photo traditions a family can keep.

Here's how to make those three minutes count — and how to turn years of first-day photos into a book that tells the story of your child growing up.

The essentials: what makes a great first-day photo

Same spot every year

This is the single most important decision. Pick a spot and stick with it. The front door, the porch steps, a specific tree in the yard, the same wall inside. When you flip through the photos side by side across years, the consistent backdrop makes the growth dramatic. Your child changes. The spot stays the same.

The sign

A sign adds information that the photo alone can't convey. Popular options:

  • Chalkboard — Reusable, classic look. Write the grade, teacher, age, and a "when I grow up" answer.
  • Letter board — Clean, modern, easy to photograph. Great for including favorites (book, subject, food).
  • Printed card — Consistent design each year. You can use the same template and just change the details.
  • Handwritten by the child — Messy, charming, and shows their handwriting evolving year to year.

What to put on the sign

  • Grade
  • Age
  • Teacher's name
  • Favorite subject
  • Best friend
  • "When I grow up I want to be..."
  • Favorite book or show

The "when I grow up" answer is the one everyone loves most. Astronaut in kindergarten, veterinarian in second grade, YouTuber in fifth grade. It tells a story all by itself.

Photo ideas beyond the basics

The backpack shot

Photograph the backpack next to your child. In kindergarten it's nearly as big as they are. By high school it looks tiny. This is one of the best growth-comparison photos you can take.

The walk-away shot

After the posed photo, capture them walking toward the school, the bus, or the car. The back-of-head, walking-away-from-you shot hits differently as the years accumulate.

The sibling lineup

If you have multiple kids, photograph them together in the same order each year. Watching the oldest tower over the youngest, then watching the youngest catch up, is its own story.

The emotional one

The nervous face before kindergarten. The too-cool-for-photos middle schooler. The bittersweet smile on the first day of senior year. Don't just photograph the posed grin — capture how they actually feel.

The parent-and-child shot

You're in this story too. Have someone snap a photo of you together on the porch. Ten years from now, you'll want to see yourself at that age, in that house, sending your kid off.

Creating a tradition book

Individual first-day photos are nice. Thirteen of them compiled into a single book is extraordinary. Here's how to structure it:

One spread per year

Give each year a two-page spread. Left page: the first-day photo with their sign. Right page: a few supporting photos from that school year — class picture, a favorite moment, an award, artwork, the last-day-of-school photo.

Keep a running interview

On the first day of school each year, ask the same questions and record the answers. Favorites, fears, ambitions, friends. Include the answers in the book. The Q&A format becomes a time capsule of who they were at each age.

Include the last day too

Bookend each school year with a first-day and last-day photo. Same spot, same pose. The difference in height, confidence, and demeanor between September and June is always visible.

What if you didn't start in kindergarten?

Start now. Whatever grade your child is in, this year's first-day photo is year one of the tradition. You can always go back and dig through old photos for previous years — they might not match the current setup, but they still belong in the book.

A tradition book that covers grades 3 through 12 is still ten years of growth. That's ten years of change, ten "when I grow up" answers, ten different backpacks. It doesn't need to be complete to be meaningful.

Making it last

The first-day photo tradition only works if the photos end up somewhere permanent. Not in a camera roll with 50,000 other photos. Not in a Google Photos album you'll forget exists. In a book — physical or digital — that you'll revisit every September when the new photo joins the collection.
