How Long Does It Take to Make a Photo Book?

November 2026 · 9 min read
Written by Olga Miller, founder of ReMeet

You sit down on a Saturday afternoon, open Shutterfly or Mixbook, and start uploading photos. Two hours later, you're on page 8 of 40, the cropping tool keeps cutting off your toddler's face, and you're wondering why you started this project at all.

Sound familiar? You're not alone. Photo book projects are one of those things that always take longer than people expect. Here's an honest breakdown of how long each step actually takes — and what your options are if you don't have a free weekend to spare.

The DIY range is wide because the time depends on two things: how many photos you have and how much you care about the result. A quick 20-page book from a single vacation might take 2-3 hours. A 100-page family yearbook from 3,000 phone photos? That's closer to 12 hours, often spread across multiple weekends because nobody has a 12-hour block to spare.

Step 1: Sorting and selecting photos (1-3 hours)

This is the step that kills most projects before they start.

The average smartphone user takes around 2,000 photos per year. A family with kids might have 5,000-10,000. If you're making a yearbook, you need to go through all of them, decide which ones are book-worthy, and sort them into some kind of order.

This means scrolling through months of camera roll, past screenshots, receipts, photos of parking spots, and thirty nearly identical shots of the same sunset. Most people find this step exhausting rather than fun. If you've been putting off making a photo book, this is probably why.

For tips on getting through this step faster, see our phone photo organization guide and digital photo declutter guide.

Step 2: Uploading (30-60 minutes)

Once you've selected your photos, you need to get them into the photo book editor. Most services let you upload from your phone, computer, or cloud storage. The upload itself takes 10-30 minutes depending on your internet speed and how many photos you're sending.

The hidden time here is in organizing after upload. Most editors dump all your photos into one big pile. You then need to sort them into the order you want, which means dragging thumbnails around and trying to remember whether the beach trip was before or after the birthday party.

Services like Google Photos can skip some of this by pulling directly from your library, but you still need to review what it picked.

Step 3: Choosing layouts (1-2 hours)

Every photo book editor gives you templates: one photo per page, two photos per page, collage grids, full-bleed spreads. The decision isn't just which template to use — it's which template works best for each specific set of photos on each page.

A landscape photo and a portrait photo don't work in the same grid. A solo hero shot deserves a full page. A series of similar moments fits a multi-photo layout. Making these decisions for 30-80 pages is where the hours quietly accumulate. Our layout tips guide covers the 12 design rules the pros use.

One of the most common photo book mistakes is using the same layout on every page because choosing different ones is too time-consuming. The result is a book that feels repetitive.

Step 4: Placing and cropping photos (2-4 hours)

This is the longest single step. You're dragging photos into placeholders, adjusting the crop so faces aren't cut off, resizing images, and nudging things until they look right.

Auto-crop features in most editors help, but they're unreliable. They center the image mathematically, not visually — which means they'll crop a group photo right through someone's forehead, or cut off a child standing at the edge of the frame. You end up manually adjusting most crops anyway.

For a 40-page book with 2-3 photos per page, that's roughly 100 individual crop decisions. At 30-60 seconds each, the time adds up fast.

Step 5: Writing captions and titles (30-60 minutes)

Captions are optional, but they're what turn a photo book from a collection of images into a story. "Beach day" doesn't say much. "The day Leo decided to eat sand for the first time" does.

Good captions take thought. You need to remember when the photo was taken, what was happening, and find a way to say it in a sentence or two. Chapter titles need to work as navigation for the book. If you skip this step, the book still works — but years from now, you'll wish you'd added context.

This is one of the things that makes a professionally designed book feel different from a DIY one. Someone who looks at your photos fresh can often write better captions than you'd expect, because they notice the details that feel ordinary to you.

Step 6: Reviewing and fixing mistakes (30-60 minutes)

Before ordering, you need to flip through every page and check for problems. Are there duplicate photos? Did a caption end up covering someone's face? Is there important content too close to the spine where it'll disappear into the gutter? Are the photos in the right order?

Most people find 5-10 issues on their first review pass. Fixing each one means navigating back to the page, adjusting the layout, and rechecking the result. Then you do another review pass. For a full list of things to watch for, see our complete photo book guide.

Step 7: Ordering and shipping (5-10 days)

Once the design is done, you choose your paper type, cover material, and size, then place the order. Most services take 5-10 business days to print and ship. Rush options from Shutterfly and Snapfish can get it to you in 3-5 days for an extra fee.

If you need a book in hand today, Walgreens offers same-day in-store pickup — but the design options are extremely basic.

Why most photo books never get finished

Here's the uncomfortable truth: the most common outcome of starting a DIY photo book isn't a finished book. It's an abandoned project sitting in a shopping cart that expires after 30 days.

The pattern is predictable. You start with enthusiasm, spend an hour uploading photos, get through 10-15 pages, realize you're not even halfway done, and close the tab to "finish later." Later turns into next weekend, then next month, then never.

This isn't a willpower problem. It's a design problem. DIY photo book editors ask you to make hundreds of micro-decisions (which photo, which crop, which layout, which caption) that individually take seconds but collectively take hours. The project is simply too large to finish in one sitting, and most people lose momentum once they stop.

The photos were important enough for you to take. They were important enough for you to start making a book. The only thing that got in the way was the process.

Which approach is right for you?

DIY makes sense when:

  • You enjoy the design process and find it relaxing
  • You have a small number of photos (under 100) from a single event
  • You want total control over every layout decision
  • Budget is more important than time

Done-for-you makes sense when:

  • You have hundreds or thousands of unsorted photos
  • You've started a photo book before and didn't finish
  • You want the book to look professionally designed
  • Your time is more valuable than the cost difference
  • You want a gift-quality book without the effort

The book that actually gets made

The biggest factor in how long a photo book takes isn't the editor you use or how many photos you have. It's whether the project gets finished at all.

Five to twelve hours of work is a real commitment. For busy parents, it means giving up multiple evenings or a full weekend. For many people, that commitment is what stands between them and a printed book of their best memories.

If you've been meaning to make a photo book for months (or years), the honest answer to "how long does it take?" might not be the hours. It might be whether you're willing to spend them. And if you're not — that's fine. There's a better way.
