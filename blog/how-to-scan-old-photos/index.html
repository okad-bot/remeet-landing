How to Scan & Digitize Old Photos at Home

September 2026 · 7 min read

Somewhere in your house there is a shoebox, a drawer, or a dusty album full of photographs that no one has looked at in years. Maybe they are your grandparents' wedding portraits, your parents' vacation snapshots from the 1970s, or your own childhood birthday parties captured on film. These prints are irreplaceable, and every year they sit untouched, they fade a little more. The good news is that digitizing them at home has never been easier or more affordable. This guide walks you through the entire process, from choosing the right scanning method to organizing your digital archive so those memories last for generations.

Flatbed Scanner vs. Phone App: Which Should You Use?

The two most accessible ways to digitize old photos are a flatbed scanner and a smartphone scanning app. Both can produce good results, but they serve different situations.

Flatbed scanners

A dedicated flatbed scanner remains the gold standard for digitizing prints. Even a mid-range model in the $80 to $200 range, such as the Epson Perfection V39 or Canon CanoScan LiDE 400, will capture fine detail, accurate color, and consistent lighting across every scan. Because the photo lies flat against the glass and the sensor moves in a controlled pass, you avoid the glare, distortion, and uneven exposure that can plague phone-based methods. If you have more than a few dozen photos to digitize, a flatbed scanner will save you time and frustration.

For negatives and slides, you will need a scanner with a built-in transparency adapter, sometimes called a film scanner unit. Models like the Epson Perfection V600 include a light source in the lid that illuminates film from behind, which is essential for capturing detail from transparencies. Dedicated film scanners such as the Plustek OpticFilm series offer even higher resolution for 35mm negatives.

Phone scanning apps

If you only have a handful of photos to digitize, or you are scanning prints at a relative's house and do not have your scanner with you, a phone app is a perfectly reasonable option. Google PhotoScan is free, works on both iOS and Android, and uses a clever multi-shot technique: you take one photo of the print, then move your phone to capture four additional reference points. The app combines these frames to remove glare and correct perspective automatically. The results are surprisingly good for casual use.

Apple's built-in document scanner in the Notes and Files apps can also capture photos, though it is designed more for documents and does not include the same glare-removal technology. Third-party apps like Photomyne and Pic Scanner Gold offer batch scanning, where you can lay several prints on a table and the app detects and crops each one individually.

The trade-off with phone apps is resolution and consistency. A modern smartphone sensor captures plenty of megapixels, but the optical quality of a phone lens, combined with variations in lighting and hand steadiness, means the results will never be as sharp or uniform as a flatbed scan. For photos you plan to turn into a printed photo book or enlarge beyond their original size, a scanner is worth the investment.

Resolution Settings: How Many DPI Do You Actually Need?

DPI stands for dots per inch, and it determines how much detail your scanner captures. Higher DPI means more data, larger files, and longer scan times, so you want to match the resolution to your intended use rather than scanning everything at the maximum setting.

Standard prints (4x6, 5x7, 8x10)

For typical photo prints that you want to view on screen, share digitally, or reprint at the same size, 300 DPI is the standard. This matches the resolution used by most commercial photo printers and produces files that are sharp at the original print dimensions. A 4x6 print scanned at 300 DPI creates an image of approximately 1200 x 1800 pixels, which is more than enough for on-screen viewing and standard reprints.

If you think you might want to enlarge the photo later, or if the original print is small (such as a wallet-size photo or a 3x3 Polaroid), scan at 600 DPI. This gives you four times the pixel count and enough headroom to enlarge to double the original size without visible quality loss.

Negatives and slides

Film negatives and slides are physically small but contain a remarkable amount of detail. A 35mm negative is only about 24 x 36 mm, so you need a much higher scanning resolution to extract that detail. Start at 2400 DPI for general use, which produces an image around 3400 x 2200 pixels from a 35mm frame. For archival purposes or large prints, scan at 4800 DPI or higher if your scanner supports it. Medium format negatives (6x6 cm or 6x7 cm) can be scanned at 1200 to 2400 DPI since the larger film area already contains more information per frame.

Large format and fragile originals

For oversize prints, posters, or very old photographs that you do not want to scan multiple times, err on the side of higher resolution. Scanning at 600 DPI creates a robust master file that you can downsample later for sharing or web use. You can always reduce resolution after the fact, but you cannot add detail that was never captured.

File Formats: TIFF, JPEG, and PNG

The file format you choose affects both image quality and file size. Here is what you need to know about each option.

TIFF (Tagged Image File Format) is the archival standard. It supports lossless compression, meaning no image data is discarded when the file is saved. TIFF files are large, often 20 to 50 MB for a single high-resolution scan, but they preserve every pixel of detail exactly as captured. If you are building a long-term digital archive, save your master scans as TIFF.

JPEG is the universal sharing format. It uses lossy compression to reduce file sizes dramatically, typically to 2 to 5 MB for a high-quality photo. The trade-off is that some image data is permanently discarded each time you save. For web sharing, email, and social media, JPEG at quality level 90 to 95 percent is indistinguishable from the original to most viewers. Just avoid re-saving a JPEG multiple times, as the quality degrades with each save cycle.

PNG offers lossless compression like TIFF but in a more web-friendly format. File sizes fall between TIFF and JPEG. PNG is a good choice if you need lossless quality but want files that are easier to share than TIFFs. However, PNG does not support CMYK color or embedded metadata as robustly as TIFF.

The recommended workflow is to scan and save master files as TIFF, then export JPEG copies for everyday use and sharing. This way you always have the highest quality original to return to. If storage space is a concern and you do not plan to do extensive editing, scanning directly to high-quality JPEG (92 percent or above) is a reasonable compromise.

Color Correction Basics

Old photos often have a color cast from age, usually a yellow or magenta tint from fading dyes. Most scanning software includes basic color correction tools, and a few simple adjustments can make a dramatic difference.

Auto color correction in your scanner software is a good starting point. It analyzes the image and attempts to neutralize color casts automatically. For many photos, this alone produces a noticeable improvement.

Levels and curves adjustments let you set the black point and white point manually. Find the darkest area of the photo that should be true black and the lightest area that should be true white, then set your levels accordingly. This stretches the tonal range and often removes color casts as a side effect.

Do not over-correct. A photo from 1965 will look unnatural if you push the colors to modern saturation levels. Aim for a result that looks like the photo did when it was new, not like it was taken with a modern digital camera. And always keep your unedited master scan so you can start over if needed.

Handling Different Photo Types

Not all old photos are standard prints, and each type requires a slightly different approach.

Glossy prints are prone to glare on a scanner. Make sure the glass is clean and the print is lying perfectly flat. If you see Newton's rings, which are rainbow-colored interference patterns caused by the glossy surface touching the glass, try placing a thin sheet of black paper behind the print.

Matte and textured prints scan more easily since they do not produce glare, but heavily textured paper can create a visible pattern in the scan. Scanning at a slightly higher resolution and then applying a mild blur can reduce this effect.

Polaroids should never be removed from their frames. The chemical layers are fragile and exposure to air accelerates degradation. Scan them as they are. Because Polaroids are small, typically about 3.1 x 3.1 inches for the image area, scan at 600 DPI to capture enough detail.

Slides and negatives require a scanner with a transparency unit, as noted earlier. For negatives, your scanner software will invert the image and attempt to remove the orange mask that is characteristic of color negative film. Check the results carefully, as automatic mask removal does not always get skin tones right.

Damaged photos with tears, creases, or water stains should be scanned as-is first, preserving the damage in the digital file. You can then use image editing software to repair the damage on a copy, keeping the unedited scan as your archival original.

Organization: Naming, Folders, and Metadata

Digitizing photos is only half the job. Without good organization, you end up with thousands of files named IMG_0001 through IMG_9999 and no way to find anything. A consistent system will save you hours of frustration later, especially when you want to organize your photo library or declutter your digital photos down the road.

File naming

Use a naming convention that sorts chronologically and includes enough context to identify the photo without opening it. A format like YYYY-MM_Description_001.tiff works well. For example: 1987-06_Beach-Vacation-Florida_003.tiff. If you do not know the exact date, use your best estimate for the year and note the uncertainty in the metadata or a companion text file.

Folder structure

Keep it simple. A structure organized by decade or year works for most family collections:

  • Photos/1960s/
  • Photos/1970s/
  • Photos/1980s/
  • Photos/Unknown-Date/

Within each folder, you can create subfolders for specific events or people if the volume warrants it. Resist the urge to create deeply nested folder hierarchies. Two or three levels deep is usually enough.

Metadata

Most image editing and management software lets you embed metadata directly into the file, including dates, locations, descriptions, and the names of people in the photo. Spending a few seconds adding this information during scanning pays off enormously when you need to search your collection later. If you are scanning with family members who can identify people and places, take advantage of their knowledge while it is available.

Preservation: Backup and Long-Term Storage

Digital files can last forever in theory, but hard drives fail, cloud services shut down, and file formats evolve. A sound backup strategy protects against all of these risks.

The 3-2-1 rule is the standard recommendation: keep three copies of your files, on two different types of storage media, with one copy stored off-site. In practice, this might mean your files live on your computer's hard drive, on an external backup drive, and in a cloud storage service like Google Drive, iCloud, or Backblaze.

External hard drives are affordable and fast for local backups. A 2 TB drive costs around $60 and can hold tens of thousands of high-resolution scans. Just remember that hard drives have a limited lifespan, typically five to ten years, so plan to migrate your data to a new drive periodically.

Cloud storage adds geographic redundancy. If your home experiences a fire, flood, or theft, the cloud copy survives. Google One, iCloud, and Amazon Photos all offer affordable plans. For archival collections, consider a service like Backblaze B2 that is designed for long-term cold storage at very low cost.

Once your photos are digitized and safely backed up, you can start thinking about what to do with old photos beyond just storing them. Sharing them with family, creating a photo book or album, or building a digital slideshow are all wonderful ways to bring those memories back to life.

Getting Started

The best time to digitize your old photos is now. Prints fade, negatives deteriorate, and the people who can identify faces and tell the stories behind the images will not be around forever. You do not need expensive equipment or technical expertise. A basic flatbed scanner or even your phone, combined with a methodical approach to resolution, file formats, and organization, is all it takes to ensure your family's visual history survives for the next generation.

Start small. Pick one album or one shoebox, work through it over a weekend, and refine your process before tackling the rest. Once you see those faded prints come to life on screen, sharp and vivid and safely backed up, you will be glad you started.
