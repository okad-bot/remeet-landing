Turn Your Kids' Artwork Into a Photo Book (And Reclaim Your Fridge)

September 2026 · 7 min read

Your child comes home from school with a painting. It goes on the fridge. Then another one. And another. Soon the fridge is three layers deep, the kitchen drawers are stuffed with construction paper masterpieces, and you have a growing pile of "too special to throw away" art that you'll definitely organize someday.

Someday is today. Here's how to turn that mountain of artwork into a photo book you'll actually look at — and finally reclaim your refrigerator.

Why a photo book beats a storage bin

Let's be honest: the bin in the closet labeled "Kids Art" is not a preservation strategy. It's a guilt reduction strategy. Nobody opens that bin. The papers wrinkle, the paint flakes, and eventually you can't tell what was drawn by your child and what was drawn by their classmate.

A photo book solves all of this:

  • The art is preserved in high quality, forever
  • It's organized by age and year, so you can see the progression
  • You can add context — what the art was supposed to be, what your child said about it, what grade they were in
  • It takes up one shelf spot instead of three storage bins
  • People actually flip through it. Including your kids.

How to photograph and scan artwork

Phone photos (quick and good enough)

For most artwork, your phone camera is perfectly fine. A few tips:

  • Lay the art flat — on a table or the floor, not held up against a wall where it curls
  • Use natural light — near a window, no flash. Flash creates glare on shiny paint and crayon
  • Shoot from directly above — hold your phone parallel to the paper to avoid perspective distortion
  • Fill the frame — get close enough that the art fills the photo with minimal background showing
  • Include a hand for scale (optional) — your child's hand next to the art adds context and charm

Scanning (for keepers)

For pieces you really want to preserve in detail — the standout paintings, the first self-portrait, the piece that made you laugh out loud — use a scanner if you have one:

  • Scan at 300 DPI minimum (600 DPI for small, detailed work)
  • Save as JPG or PNG
  • For art larger than your scanner bed, scan in sections and note the overlap

3D art and crafts

Clay sculptures, paper mache creations, popsicle stick structures — these can't be scanned but photograph well. Shoot them on a clean background from multiple angles. A white sheet of paper behind the object works surprisingly well.

Organizing by age and year

The simplest structure for a kids' art book is chronological: one section per school year or age. Within each year, you might have 10-20 pieces worth including.

Here's a framework that works:

  1. Age 2-3: Scribbles and experiments — The first intentional marks on paper. These look abstract but they're the beginning of everything.
  2. Age 4-5: Shapes emerge — The first recognizable people (circle head, stick body), houses, suns, family portraits.
  3. Age 6-7: Stories in pictures — Art starts telling stories. Scenes, characters, narratives written in uncertain letters.
  4. Age 8-10: Growing skill — More detail, more intention, more pride in the work. School projects get more ambitious.
  5. Age 11+: Personal style — Doodling in notebooks, sketching characters, experimenting with style. The art becomes more private and personal.

Adding context makes it special

A photo of a drawing is nice. A photo of a drawing with a caption that says "Age 4. This is supposed to be our dog, Max. She insisted the green part is his favorite hat." — that's a treasure.

For each piece, try to note:

  • How old your child was
  • What they said it was (in their words, especially for the early abstract phase)
  • What prompted it — a school assignment, a rainy afternoon, a birthday card for grandma
  • Any funny story behind it

Write these notes when you photograph the art, not months later. You will not remember what that purple blob was supposed to be in six months.

Keep originals vs. photograph: a practical guide

You don't need to keep every original. Here's a reasonable rule of thumb:

  • Keep the originals: First drawing with a recognizable person. First time they wrote their name. Art gifts made specifically for you. Anything with a handprint or footprint. The ones that make you emotional.
  • Photograph and recycle: School worksheets with drawings. Coloring pages. Duplicates of similar themes (the 47th rainbow). Seasonal craft projects that are more glitter than art.
  • The gray area: When in doubt, photograph it. You can always recycle the paper later. You can't un-recycle it.

Making the book

Once you have your photos organized, the book comes together quickly. A few design tips:

  • One piece of art per page for the strongest pieces; grids of 4-6 for the smaller or simpler ones
  • White or cream backgrounds work best — they let the art's colors pop without competing
  • Include photos of your child making the art, not just the finished product
  • End each year section with a candid photo of your kid at that age — it anchors the art to the person
