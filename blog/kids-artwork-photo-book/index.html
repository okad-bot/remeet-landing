The drawer problem

Kids bring home art at a steady pace. A new painting from school, a crayon drawing on Saturday, a collage from art class on Tuesday. You stick the best ones on the fridge, slide a few into a folder, and promise yourself you'll sort through them later.

Later doesn't come. The folder fills up. The paint flakes. Crayon smears across other pages. After a year or two, it's hard to tell what was drawn at age four versus age six. You feel guilty throwing anything away, but you can't store every finger painting forever.

A kids artwork photo book solves this cleanly. You photograph the artwork once. I arrange it into a book you can open and enjoy anytime. The paper originals can go. The art stays.

How to photograph kids' art at home

You don't need a scanner or special equipment. A phone camera does the job. Here's how to get clean results:

  • Lay the art flat on a table. Don't hold it up against a wall. It curls and casts shadows.
  • Use natural light. Near a window works best. No flash. Flash creates glare on shiny paint, crayon, and glitter glue.
  • Shoot from directly above. Hold your phone parallel to the paper so edges don't warp or keystone.
  • Leave a small border. Don't crop too tight. I'll clean up the framing during design.
  • Capture names and dates. If your child signed the drawing or the teacher wrote the date on the back, flip it over and photograph that too.
  • Include your child holding the art. A quick shot of them holding up the painting adds warmth. These candid photos break up the artwork pages in the final book.

For 3D crafts like clay figures, popsicle stick creations, or papier-mache animals, photograph them on a clean white background from two or three angles. I can arrange multiple views on one spread.

If you already have artwork photos in Google Photos, just share the album link. No need to download anything.

When to photograph

Timing matters more than you'd think. If you wait until there's a backlog of three years, you'll spend a whole weekend sorting and shooting. Two approaches work better:

  • Monthly batch. Pick one evening a month. Lay out whatever your child made that month, photograph it all, and drop the photos into a dedicated album. Takes about 15 minutes.
  • End of school year. Teachers often send home a full year's worth of art in June. Photograph it all in one sitting, sorted by subject or date. This is the most common approach I see from parents who order these books.

Either way, you'll have a clean album ready to send when you want the book made.

What to include beyond drawings

Crayon drawings and paintings are the obvious picks. But the best kids artwork photo books also include things parents almost throw away:

  • Finger paintings. Messy, colorful, and impossible to store without smearing. They photograph beautifully.
  • Clay and sculpture photos. Two or three angles of a clay animal on a white background look great on a spread.
  • Collages and mixed media. Glitter, fabric scraps, dried pasta glued to paper. These are fragile and fall apart over time. A photo lasts.
  • Homework with funny answers. The math worksheet where your child drew a dinosaur in the margin. The essay about what they want to be when they grow up.
  • Handwriting samples. Photograph the same child writing their name at age 3, 5, and 7. The progression is striking.
  • Handprint and footprint art. These are some of the most treasured pages in any book I design.
  • Holiday cards they made by hand. Valentine's Day cards, Mother's Day notes, birthday cards for siblings.

Don't worry about including everything. If there are twelve nearly identical rainbows, pick the best two. I can help you curate once you share the album.

Adding context to each piece

A drawing of a purple blob means nothing in ten years. A drawing of a purple blob with a caption that says "Age 4, this is our dog wearing a rocket" means everything.

When you photograph artwork, jot down a few notes:

  • The child's age or school grade
  • The date, even roughly ("Spring 2024" is fine)
  • What the child said the drawing is about
  • Any backstory ("Made this after we visited the aquarium")

Send these notes along with the photos. I add them in a clean, small typeface below each image. They turn a pretty book into a meaningful one. Years from now, those captions will be the parts you're most glad you included.

Not sure how to write good photo book captions? Keep it simple. One or two sentences per piece is plenty.

How many pages you need

This depends on how many pieces you want to include and how many years the collection spans.

  • One school year (40 to 60 pieces): 30 pages. Some pieces get a full page, others go into grids of four or six. This is enough for most single-year collections.
  • Two to three years (80 to 120 pieces): 60 to 80 pages. Each year gets its own section with a divider page.
  • Four or more years (120 to 150+ pieces): 100 pages. This is the full multi-year book that shows progression from toddler scribbles to real drawings.

If you're unsure, send what you have and I'll recommend a page count. You can also check the guide on how many photos to include.

A gift grandparents love

This is one of my most popular gift orders. Parents make the book for themselves, then order extra copies for grandparents. It's a gift that actually gets opened and displayed, not tucked away in a closet.

End-of-school-year timing works well. Collect artwork from September to June, photograph it over a weekend, and have the book printed before summer. Kids who attend daycare or preschool produce especially colorful collections because every craft project is different.

You can also make one book per year and build a series. By the time your child is a teenager, you'll have a shelf of books showing every stage. It's the kind of thing they'll want when they move out.

Frequently asked questions

How many pieces of artwork fit in one book?

A 30-page book holds roughly 40 to 60 pieces, depending on layout. Some pages show one large painting full-bleed, while others hold a grid of four or six smaller drawings. For a multi-year collection, the 100-page option ($79) fits 120 to 150 pieces comfortably.

Do I need a scanner or is a phone photo enough?

A phone photo is plenty. Lay the artwork flat on a table, use natural light from a window, and shoot from directly above. No flash. The result prints clearly at full page size. A scanner gives slightly sharper lines, but it isn't required.

Can I include 3D crafts like clay figures?

Yes. Photograph them on a clean white background from two or three angles. I can arrange multiple angles on a single spread so you see the full piece. This works for clay, papier-mache, popsicle stick creations, and anything else that doesn't lay flat.

What if the artwork spans several years?

That's the best kind of kids artwork photo book. I organize everything chronologically so you can see the progression from early scribbles to detailed drawings. Each section gets a year or age label. A 100-page book ($79) works well for multi-year collections.
