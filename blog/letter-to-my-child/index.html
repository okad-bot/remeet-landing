How to Write a Letter to Your Child (With Prompts and Examples)

September 2026 · 8 min read

You know things about your child right now that you'll forget in five years. The way they say a certain word wrong. The thing that makes them laugh until they can't breathe. The question they asked in the car that nearly made you cry.

A letter captures what a photo can't: what you were thinking, what you noticed, how it felt to be their parent in this exact moment. You don't need to be a good writer. You just need to be honest.

When to write a letter

There's no wrong time. But some moments seem to ask for it.

On their birthday

A birthday letter is the most natural tradition. Each year, you write about the year that passed — what they learned, how they changed, what made you proud. After a decade, you have a collection that traces who they became. Many parents pair this with a birthday tradition like an annual interview or "you choose the day."

At a milestone

First day of school. Losing a tooth. Learning to ride a bike. Making a team. Moving to a new house. The big moments feel significant in the moment, but the details fade fast. A short letter — even just a paragraph — anchors the memory.

Just because

Some of the best letters come on ordinary days. A Tuesday when they did something kind. A weekend when you watched them play and thought about how fast it goes. These letters carry the most emotional weight because they're not about events — they're about who your child is.

During hard times

When your child is struggling — with school, friendships, confidence, anything — a letter can say things that are hard to say out loud. "I see you working so hard." "This won't last forever." "I'm proud of you even on the hard days." They might not be ready to hear it now, but someday they'll read it and understand.

What to include

Don't overthink the structure. The best letters read like a conversation with someone you love. But if you need a framework, include some of these:

  • What they're like right now — their personality, quirks, obsessions, the way they laugh
  • A specific moment — something they said or did recently that stuck with you
  • What you've learned from them — yes, parents learn from kids too
  • What you hope for them — not achievements, but qualities: kindness, courage, curiosity
  • How it feels to be their parent — the honest version, not the greeting-card version

You don't need all five in every letter. One or two is enough.

Writing prompts to get you started

If staring at a blank page feels impossible, start with one of these:

For a birthday letter:

  • "This year, you became someone who..."
  • "The moment I'll remember most from this year is..."
  • "If I could tell you one thing about who you are at [age], it would be..."
  • "You don't know this yet, but the way you [specific thing] is going to matter when you're older."

For a milestone letter:

  • "Today you [milestone], and I want you to know what it looked like from where I was standing."
  • "I watched you walk into [school/stage/field] and I thought..."
  • "You were nervous, but you did it anyway. Here's what I saw..."

For a "just because" letter:

  • "I've been meaning to tell you something I noticed about you..."
  • "Today was ordinary, but I want to remember it because..."
  • "Right now, your favorite thing in the world is [X], and I want you to know I was paying attention."
  • "There's a version of the future where you don't remember this. So I'm writing it down."

For a letter to your daughter:

  • "The world is going to tell you a lot of things about who you should be. Here's what I want you to remember..."
  • "You remind me of [quality], and I hope you never lose that."
  • "Watching you become yourself is the privilege of my life."

For a letter to your son:

  • "Being strong doesn't mean what you think it means yet. Let me explain..."
  • "I want you to know that it's okay to [feel, cry, ask for help, change your mind]."
  • "The man I hope you become isn't about what you achieve. It's about how you treat people."

Tips for writing

  • Write like you talk. This isn't an essay. It's a letter to someone you love. Use your real voice.
  • Keep it short if you need to. Three paragraphs is a letter. One paragraph is a letter. Even a few sentences on a card counts.
  • Don't edit too much. The imperfections are what make it real. A polished letter feels like it was written for an audience. A raw one feels like it was written for them.
  • Include the date and their age. Future-you will be grateful.
  • Handwrite it if you can. Your handwriting is part of you. Someday it will be an artifact they treasure.

How to preserve your letters

The letter only matters if they can find it someday. Here are a few ways to make sure it lasts:

A physical folder or box

Simple and reliable. Keep all letters in one place — a folder, an envelope, a wooden box. Label it with their name. Store it somewhere safe but accessible. Tell your partner where it is.

A digital document

If you type faster than you write, keep a running document — one Google Doc per child, or one per year. The advantage is that you can add to it anytime, even from your phone at 11pm when the memory hits.

Inside a photo book

Some parents include letters alongside photos in an annual family yearbook or baby book. A printed page with your words next to a photo of them at that age creates something that's more than either one alone. It's context. It's the story behind the picture.

A letter jar

Write short notes throughout the year and drop them in a jar. Give the jar on a milestone birthday — 13, 16, 18. They'll sit on the floor and read sixty notes from the people who love them most.

You don't have to start at the beginning

If your child is already seven and you've never written a letter, don't feel guilty. Start now. Write about who they are today. You don't need to catch up. There's no backlog to fill. The letter you write tonight is the one that matters.

And if you want to make it a tradition, pair it with their birthday. One letter per year. In ten years, you'll have ten letters. That's a collection worth more than anything you could buy them.
