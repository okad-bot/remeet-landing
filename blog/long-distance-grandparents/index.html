How to Keep Kids Connected With Long-Distance Grandparents

September 2026 · 8 min read

Grandparents matter. Studies consistently show that kids who have close relationships with their grandparents have better emotional health, stronger social skills, and a deeper sense of belonging. But when grandparents live hundreds or thousands of miles away, maintaining that bond takes intentional effort.

The good news: distance doesn't have to mean distance. With some planning and consistency, long-distance grandparents can be a real, regular presence in your kids' lives — not just the people who visit twice a year.

Video calls that actually work

The default long-distance tool is the video call. But if your current approach is a stilted FaceTime where your toddler stares at the ceiling and your mother-in-law asks "can you see me?" for the third time, there's room to improve.

Make it routine, not random

Set a regular time — Sunday morning, Wednesday after school, Saturday before lunch. When the call is predictable, kids start expecting it. It becomes part of the week, not an interruption. Grandparents can plan around it too. A standing appointment feels different than a surprise ring.

Give it structure

Young kids don't do well with open-ended "so what's new?" conversations. Instead, try:

  • Show and tell: Have them show grandparents something — a drawing, a toy, a fort, their missing tooth
  • Read together: Grandparent reads a book aloud over video. The child follows along with the same book
  • Cook together: Both make the same simple recipe at the same time, on video
  • Play a game: Card games (each has their own deck), 20 questions, I Spy around the room, drawing games
  • Give a tour: Walk them through the house, the yard, the classroom art on the fridge

Keep it short

A five-year-old's attention span for a video call is about ten minutes. That's fine. A short, fun call where they laughed is better than a long one where they squirmed. Let grandparents know that short is okay — it's not a rejection, it's just being five.

Don't force it

Some days kids aren't in the mood. That's okay. The grandparent can still talk to the parent. The child hears the conversation in the background and absorbs the relationship, even without participating directly.

Shared activities across the miles

Doing something together — even remotely — creates a different kind of connection than just talking.

The same book

Grandparent and grandchild read the same book, then talk about it on their next call. Works best with chapter books for older kids, or picture books that grandparents can read aloud on video for younger ones.

Matching projects

Send the same craft kit, puzzle, or building set to both locations. Work on it "together" over several calls. A 500-piece puzzle that takes three weeks of Sunday calls to finish creates an ongoing shared project that gives every call a purpose.

Pen pal cooking

Grandparent sends a family recipe. Grandchild tries to make it (with parental help). They compare results on the next call. Over time, this builds a collection of family recipes passed down in real time, not discovered in a box someday.

Garden buddies

Both plant the same seeds at the same time. Compare growth on each call. Kids love checking whose sunflower is taller. The plant becomes a physical reminder of the grandparent between calls.

Sending photos (and making them matter)

Most grandparents get photos through text messages or social media. They see them, smile, scroll on. The photos disappear into the feed. Here's how to make photo sharing more meaningful:

A shared album

Create a shared Google Photos or Apple Photos album. Add photos throughout the week — not just milestones, but the mundane stuff. Breakfast faces. The walk to school. Their current obsession. Grandparents who see the everyday feel more connected than those who only see the highlights.

Weekly photo updates

If a shared album feels like too much, send a weekly text with 3-5 photos and a few sentences of context. "This week: she lost another tooth, they built a fort that lasted three days, and the cat sat on his homework." Context turns photos into stories.

A grandparent photo book

Once a year — at the holidays or on Grandparents' Day — collect the best photos from the year and make a photo book for grandparents. This is the gift they'll keep on the coffee table and show to every friend who visits. It's a physical, tangible connection to grandchildren they can't hold every day.

Include captions with ages, funny quotes, and milestone updates. Grandparents want the details. "Emma, age 4, explaining why dogs are better than cats" means more than a photo without context.

Letter writing

In an age of instant communication, a physical letter in the mailbox is surprisingly powerful.

For young kids

Even before they can write, kids can draw a picture and "sign" it with a scribble. Mail it in an envelope. Grandparents will keep these forever. As they get older, they can write a few sentences, then paragraphs. The habit builds gradually.

For grandparents

Encourage grandparents to write back. A letter from grandma or grandpa — with their handwriting, their stories, their personality — becomes a keepsake. Some grandparents include small things: a pressed flower, a newspaper clipping, a photo from their own childhood.

Make it easy

Keep pre-stamped, pre-addressed envelopes in a drawer. When a child draws something or writes something, the envelope is ready. Remove the friction and it happens more often.

Making visits count

When you do visit — or when grandparents come to you — make the most of it.

Create visit traditions

Something you always do when you're together: the same restaurant, the same park, the same game, the same recipe. Kids anticipate traditions, and traditions create belonging. "We always make pancakes at Grandma's house" is a sentence that builds identity.

Take photos together

Not just the obligatory everyone-look-at-the-camera photo (although take that too). Capture the candid moments: reading together on the couch, cooking side by side, walking hand in hand. These natural photos become the most meaningful ones over time.

Record stories

Ask grandparents to tell a story from their own childhood while you're together. Record it on your phone. Kids love hearing that Grandpa got in trouble at school too. And someday, when that voice is only a memory, you'll have it preserved.

Leave something behind

Have kids leave a drawing, a note, or a small project at the grandparents' house. Something they see every day between visits. And have grandparents send kids home with something small — a recipe card, a photo from the visit, a note in their suitcase.

Technology that helps

Beyond video calls, a few tools can bridge the gap:

  • Digital photo frames: Load one with photos for the grandparent's house. Set it to update automatically when you add new photos to a shared album. They see new pictures without having to open an app.
  • Voice message apps: Voice messages feel more personal than text and are easier than a full call. Kids can send a 15-second message ("Grandma, I got a 100 on my spelling test!") anytime.
  • Shared family calendars: Let grandparents see what's on the schedule — soccer games, school plays, doctor visits. They can text "good luck at your game today!" which means the world to a kid who thinks no one besides their parents is watching.

The real work is consistency

The specific activities matter less than the consistency. A weekly call that happens every week beats a grand gesture once a year. A photo sent every Sunday beats a photo dump every six months. Kids build relationships through repetition and reliability, and grandparents stay connected through the steady stream of small updates.

It takes effort, mostly from the parents who are managing the logistics. But the payoff — a child who feels known and loved by their grandparents, regardless of distance — is worth every scheduled call and stamped envelope.

Years from now, your child won't remember whether grandma lived ten minutes away or ten hours away. They'll remember whether she was part of their life.
