How to Create a Memorial Photo Book: A Gentle Guide

September 2026 · 11 min read

Creating a photo book for someone you've lost is one of the most meaningful things you can make. It's also one of the hardest to start. The photos bring comfort and grief at the same time. The project can feel overwhelming when you're already exhausted.

This guide is here to help you through it, one step at a time. There's no rush. There's no wrong way. The goal is simply to gather the photos and memories that matter and put them in a form that your family can hold, share, and return to whenever they need to.

When to start

There's no right time. Some people begin within weeks because the project gives them something constructive to focus on. Others wait months or years until the memories bring more warmth than pain. Both are fine.

If you're not ready to look through photos yet, you can start with just one step: asking family members to send you their photos. You don't have to open them right away. Just collect them in one place so they're there when you're ready.

Gathering photos from family

One of the most beautiful parts of creating a memorial book is that it brings together photos no one person has. Your aunt has photos from the 1970s. Your cousin has holiday gatherings from the 2000s. Your sibling has phone photos from last year. Each person holds a piece of the story.

How to ask

A simple message works best. Something like: "I'm putting together a photo book to remember [name]. If you have any photos of them — from any time, any quality — would you share them with me? Phone photos, scanned prints, anything." Most people will be glad to contribute. It gives them a way to help when they may not know what else to do.

How to collect

Create a shared folder (Google Drive, Dropbox, or iCloud) where people can upload their photos. This is easier than having everyone text or email photos individually, which quickly becomes hard to track. Name the folder clearly and share the link with family members.

Old printed photos

Some of the best photos will be physical prints — faded, creased, tucked inside drawers and albums. These are worth including. You can scan them with a phone scanning app (Google PhotoScan works well) or a flatbed scanner. The scans don't need to be perfect. A slightly tilted scan of a 1960s snapshot has its own kind of beauty in a memorial book.

Organizing by life chapters

A memorial photo book works best when it follows the arc of a life rather than appearing as a random collection. You don't need photos from every year, but a chapter structure gives the book shape and meaning.

A natural chapter structure might look like:

  • Early years — Childhood photos, family of origin, school pictures
  • Young adulthood — College, early career, friendships, adventures
  • Family — Marriage, children, building a home
  • Passions — Hobbies, travels, the things that brought them joy
  • Gatherings — Holidays, birthdays, reunions, celebrations
  • Recent years — The most recent chapter, however long or short

You may not have photos for every chapter, and that's okay. Some chapters might be a single page. Others might be ten pages. Let the photos you have guide the structure rather than trying to fill gaps that don't exist.

Including memorabilia

A memorial book doesn't have to be only photographs. Scanned documents and personal items can add depth and intimacy that photos alone can't provide.

Consider including:

  • Handwritten notes or letters — Scan a letter they wrote, a birthday card, a note they left on the fridge. Their handwriting is a powerful presence on a page.
  • Recipes — If they were known for a particular dish, scan the recipe card — especially if it's in their handwriting, with flour stains and annotations.
  • Documents — A military ID, a diploma, a passport photo, a newspaper clipping. These artifacts place a life in its time.
  • Artwork or crafts — Children's drawings they kept, paintings they made, quilts they sewed. Photograph these items and include them.
  • Screenshots of messages — A text conversation, a voicemail transcription, a social media post. These feel informal, but they're often the most recent and personal records we have.

These items work best when placed alongside related photos rather than in a separate section. A scanned recipe on the same spread as a photo of them cooking tells a fuller story than either element alone.

Writing captions and text

Words in a memorial book carry special weight. They don't need to be eloquent or long. The simplest captions are often the most powerful.

What to write

  • Context the photo can't show. "Thanksgiving 1998 — the year she burned the turkey and we ordered pizza. She laughed about it for years."
  • What you remember about the moment. Not a description of the photo, but what you felt, heard, or noticed.
  • Things they used to say. Phrases, expressions, advice they repeated. "She always said, 'If you're going to do it, do it right.'"
  • Who the people in the photo are. Future generations looking at this book won't know. Label relationships: "With her sister Ruth, summer 1972."

What to avoid

  • Don't feel pressured to write something for every photo. Some images speak for themselves.
  • Don't worry about being a good writer. Honest and simple is better than polished and distant.
  • Don't include only the highlights. The everyday moments — reading in a chair, walking the dog, sitting at the kitchen table — are often what people miss most.

Including others' words

Ask family members if they'd like to contribute a short memory, a sentence, or even just a word that comes to mind when they think of this person. These contributions make the book a collective act of remembrance rather than one person's project.

Involving family members

A memorial book can be a solitary project or a shared one. Both approaches have value, but involving others often makes the book richer and the process more healing.

Ways to involve family:

  • Photo gathering — As described above, ask everyone to contribute photos. This is the easiest entry point.
  • Caption writing — Ask specific people to write captions for photos they were in. "Uncle David, can you tell me what was happening in this photo from the fishing trip?"
  • Photo selection — Share a folder of candidate photos and ask family members to mark their favorites. This helps you see which images resonate most.
  • Review — Before printing, share the proof with close family members. They may catch a mislabeled date, suggest a missing photo, or want to adjust a caption.

Be prepared: looking through photos together can be emotional. That's not a problem to solve. It's part of what makes the project meaningful.

Design considerations

A memorial book benefits from simplicity. This isn't the place for busy backgrounds, decorative borders, or playful fonts. Let the photos and words be the focus.

  • Use a neutral background. White, cream, or soft gray. Nothing that competes with the images.
  • Keep layouts clean. One to three photos per page. Give each image room. For more on layout principles, see our photo book layout tips.
  • Choose a classic font. A quiet serif for text, simple and readable. Nothing trendy.
  • Include breathing room. Pages with just a quote, a date, or a chapter title give the reader space to pause and reflect.
  • Consider the cover. A portrait photo works well. So does a simple photo of their hands, their garden, their front door — something that feels like them without being a formal portrait.

Choosing a format

For a memorial book, a hardcover is worth the investment. This is a book that will be passed between family members, placed on coffee tables, and kept for generations. It should feel substantial and lasting.

A landscape or square format often works better than portrait for memorial books, because it accommodates both horizontal and vertical photos without awkward cropping.

Aim for 40-80 pages. A memorial book doesn't need to be encyclopedic. A thoughtfully curated 50-page book is more meaningful than a 200-page book with every photo ever taken.

Making copies

Consider ordering multiple copies. Siblings, children, grandchildren, and close friends may each want their own. A memorial book makes a profoundly meaningful gift — it says "this person mattered, and so do you."

You might also keep a digital version accessible to family members who live far away or who want to look through it without handling a physical copy.

It doesn't have to be perfect

The most important thing about a memorial photo book is that it exists. It doesn't need perfect scans, professional photos, or beautiful writing. It needs the real photos, the real memories, the real person.

A blurry phone photo of them laughing at dinner last year belongs in this book just as much as a perfect studio portrait from decades ago. Maybe more. Because it's recent, and real, and it's how you actually remember them — not posed, not perfect, just present.

If you're struggling to get started, begin with five photos. Just five that you love. Put them in a folder. That's your book's foundation. The rest will follow when you're ready.
