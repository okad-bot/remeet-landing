Moving to a New City: The Complete Family Checklist

September 2026 · 11 min read

Moving is stressful. Moving with kids is a different kind of stressful — the kind where you're managing logistics, emotions, schools, and your own anxiety all at once. Your to-do list has a hundred items, and you keep thinking of new ones at 2 AM.

This checklist won't make moving easy. But it will make it organized. Everything you need to handle, broken into three phases: before you go, during the move, and after you arrive.

Before you go: Make a photo book of where you've been

Before you get buried in boxes and logistics, do this one thing while you still can: document the place you're leaving.

Walk around your neighborhood with your phone. Photograph the house, the front door, the kids' rooms, the backyard. The school. The park where your kids learned to ride bikes. The coffee shop you went to every Saturday. The neighbors' houses. The view from the kitchen window.

These are the things you think you'll remember but won't — at least not clearly. A year from now, your kids will want to see what their old room looked like. Five years from now, you'll want to remember the street.

Take those photos, add the ones already on your phone from the last few years in this place, and make a photo book. Not after the move when you're unpacking and exhausted — now, while you're still here and can fill in the gaps. It becomes a goodbye gift to yourselves, a chapter marker in your family's story.

Before you go: The practical checklist

Housing (8-12 weeks before)

  • Research neighborhoods. Prioritize: commute time, school quality, walkability, safety, and proximity to parks/activities for kids.
  • If renting, start searching early — good family rentals go fast in most cities.
  • If buying, connect with a local real estate agent who knows family-friendly areas.
  • If possible, visit in person before committing. Drive the neighborhood at different times of day.
  • Check for registered sex offenders nearby (familywatchdog.us or local registries).

Schools (8-12 weeks before)

  • Research school districts and individual schools. GreatSchools.org gives ratings, but also read parent reviews and check test scores.
  • Contact target schools to understand enrollment requirements and deadlines.
  • Request school records and transcripts from current school.
  • Get copies of IEPs, 504 plans, or any special education documentation.
  • Ask about orientation programs for new students.
  • For preschoolers: research daycare and preschool waitlists (some have 6+ month waits).

Healthcare (6-8 weeks before)

  • Find a new pediatrician. Ask for recommendations in local parent groups or on Nextdoor.
  • Find a new family doctor or general practitioner.
  • Transfer prescriptions to a pharmacy near your new home.
  • Get copies of medical records, vaccination records, and dental records for every family member.
  • If anyone is in ongoing treatment (therapy, orthodontics, specialists), arrange referrals to providers in the new city.
  • Check if your insurance covers providers in the new area. If not, research new plans.

Logistics (4-8 weeks before)

  • Get moving quotes from at least three companies. For long-distance, book early — summer weekends fill up months in advance.
  • Decide what to move, sell, donate, or throw away. Moving is expensive per pound — this is the time to declutter.
  • File a change of address with USPS (takes effect 7-10 business days).
  • Update your address with: bank, credit cards, insurance, subscriptions, employer, voter registration, DMV.
  • Transfer or cancel utilities at current home (electric, gas, water, internet, trash).
  • Set up utilities at new home. Schedule installation dates for before you arrive if possible.
  • Forward important documents: tax records, legal papers, passport (update address).

For the kids (4-6 weeks before)

  • Tell them early and honestly. "We're moving to [city] because [reason]." Don't surprise them.
  • Let them feel their feelings. Anger, sadness, excitement, fear — all of it is valid.
  • Research the new city together. Find the parks, the pools, the ice cream shops, the cool things to do. Give them something to look forward to.
  • Plan goodbye activities with friends. Playdates, sleepovers, a small going-away party.
  • For older kids: help them exchange contact information with friends. Video calls keep friendships alive.
  • Let each child pack a "first night" bag with comfort items: favorite stuffed animal, blanket, book, game.

During the move

Moving day

  • If possible, arrange for kids to be with a friend or family member on moving day. The chaos of movers, boxes, and an emptying house can be overwhelming.
  • Do a final walkthrough of every room, closet, cabinet, and the garage.
  • Check the attic, basement, and outdoor shed — the most forgotten items live here.
  • Read all meters (electric, gas, water) and photograph them for final billing.
  • Leave keys, garage openers, and any notes for the new owners/tenants.
  • Take one last photo of the house. The empty rooms look strange, but you'll be glad you have it.

The drive (or flight)

  • Pack a car bag with essentials: snacks, water, chargers, medications, documents, change of clothes, entertainment for kids.
  • If driving long distance, plan stops every 2-3 hours. Let kids run around. Everyone needs breaks.
  • Keep important documents (lease, school records, medical records, IDs) with you — not in the moving truck.
  • If flying: ship a few boxes ahead so you have basics when you arrive.

After you arrive: The first week

Setting up the house

  • Set up kids' rooms first. Having their own space ready — bed made, toys accessible, familiar things visible — gives them an anchor in the chaos.
  • Set up the kitchen next. Being able to cook a meal (even a simple one) makes a house feel like home faster than anything.
  • Don't try to unpack everything in one weekend. Do one room per day. Living with boxes for a week is fine.
  • Prioritize: beds, bathroom, kitchen, then everything else.

Immediate tasks

  • Change locks or rekey the new home.
  • Test all smoke detectors and carbon monoxide detectors.
  • Locate the circuit breaker, water shutoff, and gas shutoff.
  • Meet your immediate neighbors. A simple "Hi, we just moved in, we have two kids" goes a long way.
  • Find the nearest: hospital/urgent care, pharmacy, grocery store, gas station.

After you arrive: The first month

Settling in as a family

  • Explore the neighborhood together. Walk to the park, find the playground, ride bikes on the new streets. Make it an adventure, not an adjustment.
  • Establish new routines quickly. Same bedtime, same morning routine, same family dinner. Routines are anchors for kids.
  • Find one "regular" spot — a coffee shop, a bakery, a playground — and go there weekly. Familiarity builds faster than you think.
  • Stock the pantry with favorite foods. Small comforts matter during big transitions.

Schools and activities

  • Complete school enrollment. Bring: proof of residence, birth certificate, vaccination records, previous school records, IEP/504 if applicable.
  • Meet the teacher before the first day if possible. A familiar face makes everything less scary.
  • Walk or drive the school route with your child before the first day. Knowing the path reduces anxiety.
  • Sign up for one extracurricular activity. Sports, art, music, scouts — anything that introduces them to other kids outside school.
  • Volunteer at school. It helps you meet other parents, and your kids see you investing in their new world.

Making friends (for parents too)

  • Join local parent groups (Facebook groups, Nextdoor, Meetup, school PTA).
  • Say yes to every invitation for the first three months. Even if you're tired. Especially if you're tired.
  • Invite a neighbor or school parent for coffee. Most people won't make the first move — be the one who does.
  • Find your community: a gym, a church, a running club, a book club, a volunteer organization. Adults need structured social settings just as much as kids do.
  • Be patient. It takes about 6-12 months to build a social circle in a new city. That's normal.

Helping kids adjust

  • Watch for signs of struggle: changes in sleep, appetite, mood, school performance, or withdrawal from activities.
  • Don't dismiss their feelings. "I know you miss your old friends. That makes sense. It's okay to feel sad and excited at the same time."
  • Help them stay connected with old friends through video calls, texts, and letters.
  • Plan a visit back to the old city if feasible — or plan for old friends to visit. Having it on the calendar helps.
  • Give it time. Most kids adjust within 3-6 months, though it can take a full school year to truly feel settled.

After you arrive: The first year

Administrative tasks (first 30-90 days)

  • Get new driver's licenses (most states require this within 30-90 days).
  • Register vehicles in the new state.
  • Register to vote at your new address.
  • Update your estate planning documents (wills, trusts) if you changed states — laws vary.
  • File a change of address with the IRS if needed.

Making it home

  • Start new family traditions specific to your new city. A Sunday hike, a Friday pizza spot, a seasonal festival. These become the story of this chapter.
  • Explore beyond your neighborhood. Visit museums, parks, farmers markets, and local landmarks. Become a tourist in your own city.
  • Take photos. A lot of them. The new house, the new school, the new friends, the new favorite spots. In five years, you'll want to remember what "new" looked like.

The emotional side nobody talks about

Moving checklists focus on logistics because logistics are solvable. But the harder part is emotional: the grief of leaving a place you loved, the disorientation of being somewhere unfamiliar, the exhaustion of rebuilding a social life from scratch.

It's normal to feel lonely for the first few months. It's normal for kids to say "I hate it here" and mean it — temporarily. It's normal to miss your old grocery store, your old neighbors, your old commute.

It gets better. The new place becomes home not all at once, but gradually — when you find your coffee shop, when your kid makes a friend, when a neighbor waves and knows your name.

And years from now, when your kids are older and this city is the one they call home, you'll be glad you had photos of both places. The one you left and the one you found. A family yearbook that captures the before and after tells the story of a family that was brave enough to start over. That's worth holding onto.
