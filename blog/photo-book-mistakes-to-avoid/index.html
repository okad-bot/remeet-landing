10 Photo Book Mistakes That Ruin the Final Result

November 2026 · 10 min read
Written by Olga Miller, founder of ReMeet

Making a photo book should be one of the most satisfying projects you ever start. You've got thousands of photos. You've got the memories. All you need is to put them together into something beautiful.

But somewhere between choosing the first photo and clicking "order," things go wrong. Pages feel cluttered. Photos look blurry. The whole book feels … off.

These aren't rookie mistakes. They're the same ones that trip up everyone, from first-time parents making a family yearbook to seasoned scrapbookers switching to digital. The good news: every one of them is fixable.

Here are the 10 most common photo book mistakes — and exactly how to avoid them.

1. Including every photo instead of curating

This is where most photo book projects go off the rails. You have 3,000 photos from the year. You can't pick, so you include 800. The result is a 400-page book where every moment gets a tiny thumbnail and nothing stands out.

A great photo book isn't a backup drive. It's a story. And stories need editing.

How to fix it: For a 100-page book, aim for 150 to 250 photos. Start by removing duplicates, blurry shots, and screenshots. Then do a second pass and ask: "Does this photo add something new?" Five great beach photos beat twenty mediocre ones. If you need help organizing your photos first, our phone photo organization guide walks through the process.

With a done-for-you service, curation is handled for you — you share the full album, and the design team picks the strongest shots.

2. Ignoring chronological order

When you import photos from different devices, cloud folders, or shared albums, they often land in random order. January's birthday ends up next to August's vacation. The book jumps around in time, and flipping through it feels disorienting rather than nostalgic.

How to fix it: Sort everything by date before you start designing. Most editors let you sort imported photos by date taken. Group related moments together — a trip, a birthday, a holiday — and give each group its own section. This natural timeline is what makes a photo book feel like a story rather than a shuffled deck of cards.

If your photos are scattered across Google Photos, iCloud, and old hard drives, decluttering your digital photos first saves hours of sorting later.

3. Bad cropping

Auto-crop is convenient — until it cuts off someone's forehead, chops a hand at the wrist, or centers on the background while the actual subject sits in a corner. Most template-based editors apply a default crop when you drop a photo into a frame, and many people never adjust it.

The printed result: faces cut in half, toddlers missing feet, group shots where the person on the edge is cropped out entirely.

How to fix it: After placing every photo, zoom in and check the crop. Make sure no one's head is cut off, there's a little breathing room above the tallest person, and the subject is actually the focus of the frame. For vertical photos in horizontal frames (or vice versa), consider changing the frame shape instead of forcing a crop that loses half the image.

This is one of the biggest advantages of professional design: at ReMeet, every photo is cropped by hand. Nobody gets cut off, and there's always room above their heads.

4. Using the same layout on every page

Most photo book editors offer dozens of layout templates. Most people pick one they like and use it on every single page. The result is a book that feels monotonous by page 10. The eye has nothing new to land on, and every spread blends into the next.

How to fix it: Vary your layouts. Alternate between full-page photos, two-photo spreads, and multi-photo collage pages. Give your best photos a full page to themselves. Follow a busy spread with a quiet one — a single photo with lots of white space. Our photo book layout guide covers 12 pro design rules that make any book look better.

Think of it like pacing in a movie: fast scenes need quiet scenes to breathe. A page with six photos needs a page with one.

5. Skipping captions and chapter titles

When you're making the book, every photo is obvious. That's your backyard. That's the restaurant from Tuesday. That's your partner's college roommate.

Five years from now, you won't remember any of it. Ten years from now, your kids won't know who that person is. The photos lose their meaning without context.

How to fix it: Add captions. They don't need to be long — "Beach trip, May 2026" or "Ella's first steps" is enough. Use chapter titles to mark big moments: trips, holidays, birthdays. These small labels turn a collection of images into a narrative. A good photo book guide will walk you through what to include.

At ReMeet, we draft short handwritten-style captions based on what's in your photos. You can change them, remove them, or keep them as they are.

6. Using low-resolution photos

A photo looks great on your phone's retina screen. You add it to the book, order, and when the print arrives … it's blurry. Pixelated. Soft in all the wrong places.

This usually happens when people use photos saved from Instagram, received through WhatsApp or Messenger, downloaded from Facebook, or screenshotted from someone else's feed. Every one of these methods compresses the image dramatically. A 3,000-pixel original becomes an 800-pixel copy that looks fine at phone size but terrible in print.

How to fix it: Always use original photos from your camera roll, Google Photos at full resolution, or iCloud originals. If someone sent you a photo through a messaging app, ask them to share it via Google Drive, AirDrop, or a shared album instead. For print, you need at least 2,000 × 3,000 pixels for a full-page image in a standard book.

Check your editor's resolution warnings — most will flag low-resolution images with a yellow or red indicator. Don't ignore them.

7. Forgetting about the gutter

The gutter is the center crease of the book where the pages meet the spine. Any photo or text placed across the gutter will be partially hidden in the fold. Faces disappear into the crease. Text becomes unreadable. A panoramic photo that spans two pages loses its center third.

How to fix it: Keep important elements — especially faces and text — away from the center crease. If you want a full-bleed panoramic spread, accept that some of the middle will be lost and make sure nothing critical is there. Some paper types like layflat pages minimize this problem because the book opens completely flat.

Most template editors show a gray zone near the gutter — use it as a guide. If your editor doesn't, add 10-15mm of margin on the inside edge of each page.

8. Cramming too many photos per page

When you have a lot of photos and limited pages, the temptation is to fit five or six on every spread. The result: every image is thumbnail-sized, there's no breathing room, and the book feels exhausting to look at. None of the photos get the attention they deserve.

How to fix it: Limit most pages to 1-3 photos. Use white space generously — it's not wasted space, it's what makes the photos stand out. If you're worried about fitting everything, either add more pages or (better) go back to mistake #1 and cull more aggressively.

Consider your book size too. A small 6×6 book can only fit one or two photos per page before it feels cramped. A larger 10×10 or 12×12 format gives you more room for multi-photo layouts.

9. Using clashing fonts and decorations

Stickers, clip art, glitter borders, five different fonts in five different colors. What looks fun in the editor looks chaotic in print. Every decorative element competes with your photos for attention — and your photos lose.

How to fix it: Pick one or two fonts for the entire book: one for titles and one for captions. Stick to neutral colors — black, dark gray, or white text depending on the background. Skip the clip art entirely. The best photo books look like magazines: clean layouts, calm typography, and photos as the star.

10. Never actually finishing the project

This is the biggest mistake of all, and it's the one nobody talks about.

You start the project on a Saturday afternoon. You upload 600 photos. You design 12 pages. Then life happens. The project sits in your drafts for weeks, months, sometimes years. You tell yourself you'll finish it next weekend. You never do.

Meanwhile, 2,000 new photos pile up on your phone, and the project feels even more overwhelming than before.

How to fix it: Set a deadline. Give yourself one weekend — not a month. Accept that it won't be perfect. A finished photo book with imperfect layouts is infinitely better than a perfect photo book that only exists in your drafts.

Or skip the work entirely. The reason done-for-you services exist is precisely this: most people never finish DIY projects. You share a link to your photo album, and 72 hours later you get a designed proof. No editor to learn, no templates to pick, no layouts to arrange.

Quick reference: all 10 mistakes

  1. Including too many photos — curate, don't dump
  2. Random order — sort by date before you start
  3. Bad cropping — check every photo after placing it
  4. Same layout on every page — vary the pacing
  5. No captions or titles — add context for future you
  6. Low-resolution images — use originals, not social media saves
  7. Content in the gutter — keep faces away from the spine
  8. Too many photos per page — use white space generously
  9. Clashing fonts and decorations — less is more
  10. Never finishing — set a deadline, or let someone else do it
