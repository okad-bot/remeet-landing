Photo Book Sizes Explained: Which One to Choose

September 2026 · 7 min read

You've decided to make a photo book. You've chosen your photos, maybe even thought about the layout. Then the size options appear: 8x8, 8x10, 10x10, 11x8.5, 11x14, 12x12. What do these numbers mean, which one is right for your project, and does it actually matter?

It matters more than you'd think. The size of your book affects how your photos are displayed, how many images fit on each page, how the book feels in your hands, and how it looks on a shelf or coffee table. This guide walks through every common size so you can choose with confidence.

How photo book sizes work

Photo book sizes are measured in inches, with width listed first and height second. An 8x10 book is 8 inches wide and 10 inches tall (portrait orientation). A 10x8 book is 10 inches wide and 8 inches tall (landscape orientation). A 10x10 book is square.

Three orientation categories cover nearly every photo book:

  • Square (8x8, 10x10, 12x12) — Equal width and height. Versatile, modern, and works with both portrait and landscape photos.
  • Landscape (10x8, 11x8.5, 13x10) — Wider than tall. Natural for horizontal photos like landscapes, travel, and group shots.
  • Portrait (8x10, 8x11, 10x13) — Taller than wide. Natural for vertical photos like portraits, standing poses, and architectural shots.

The orientation of your book should generally match the orientation of most of your photos. If 70% of your images are landscape, a landscape-format book will display them better. If you have a roughly even mix, square is the safest choice.

Common sizes and their best uses

8x8 inches (small square)

The compact square format. This size feels intimate and personal — more like a journal than a coffee table book. It's easy to hold in one hand, fits on a bookshelf without standing out, and is light enough to mail without excessive shipping costs.

Best for: Personal journals, yearly recaps, single-event books (a birthday party, a weekend trip), gift books where portability matters, and books for children to handle.

Photo capacity: 1-2 photos per page comfortably. Full-page images work well but multi-photo layouts can feel cramped. Plan for 40-80 photos across 40-60 pages.

Price range: The most affordable size at most services. A good entry point if you're making your first photo book.

8x10 or 8x11 inches (medium portrait)

The classic portrait format, similar to a hardcover novel. This size feels natural in your hands and works well for books with a lot of vertical images — portraits, standing group shots, and architectural photography.

Best for: Portrait collections, baby books (babies are often photographed vertically), yearbooks, and books with substantial text alongside photos.

Photo capacity: 1-3 photos per page. The vertical orientation is forgiving — a single portrait fills a page beautifully, and two landscape images can stack vertically with room for a caption.

Price range: Moderate. Slightly more than an 8x8 but still within budget-friendly territory.

10x8 or 11x8.5 inches (medium landscape)

The landscape counterpart to the portrait format. This is the aspect ratio of a laptop screen or a traditional photo print, and landscape-oriented photos fill these pages naturally.

Best for: Travel books, landscape photography, road trip journals, and any collection heavy on horizontal images.

Photo capacity: 1-3 photos per page. Landscape photos get maximum impact at full width, and you can stack a smaller landscape image above or below a featured shot.

Price range: Similar to the portrait format. The larger surface area compared to an 8x8 is reflected in a modest price increase.

10x10 inches (medium square)

The versatile middle ground. This is probably the most popular photo book size overall, and for good reason: it's large enough to showcase photos beautifully but compact enough to hold comfortably and store easily. It handles both landscape and portrait images gracefully.

Best for: Everything. Family yearbooks, event books, general-purpose photo books, and any project where your images are a mix of orientations.

Photo capacity: 2-4 photos per page without feeling crowded. Full-page images have real presence, and multi-photo spreads give you room to tell a story. Plan for 60-120 photos across 40-60 pages.

Price range: Mid-range. The sweet spot between cost and impact for most people.

The most popular choice

If you're unsure which size to pick, 10x10 is the safest bet. It works with every photo orientation, handles multi-photo layouts gracefully, looks substantial on a coffee table, and fits on a standard bookshelf. Most photo book services optimize their templates for this size.

11x14 or 12x12 inches (large format)

The statement piece. Large-format photo books command attention. They're meant to be displayed, not shelved. When you open an 11x14 book, a full-page landscape photo spans nearly two feet of visual space — it's closer to looking at a framed print than a photo in a book.

Best for: Coffee table books, wedding albums, professional photography portfolios, landscape collections, and any project where the photos themselves are the centerpiece.

Photo capacity: 1-4 photos per page with generous spacing. At this size, single images per page feel intentional and dramatic. Multi-photo layouts have room to breathe without any image feeling small.

Price range: Premium. Large-format books typically cost 50-100% more than medium sizes due to paper costs, printing, and shipping weight. They're worth it for special projects but not necessary for everyday books.

5x5 or 6x6 inches (mini books)

Mini photo books are smaller than a standard paperback. They're impulse-gift territory — cute, inexpensive, and easy to produce quickly. They won't replace a full-size book, but they serve a different purpose.

Best for: Stocking stuffers, party favors, wallet-size brag books, quick gifts for coworkers, "best of" highlights from a larger collection. Also useful as prototypes: if you're unsure about a layout, printing a mini version first lets you test the design cheaply.

Photo capacity: 1 photo per page, full bleed. These are essentially a stack of photo prints bound together. Captions and text don't work well at this scale.

Price range: Very affordable. Often under $20. Some services offer packs of multiples at a discount.

How size affects your layout

The size you choose doesn't just determine the physical dimensions — it shapes your design options. Here's how. For more detailed guidance, see our photo book layout tips.

Photos per page

Larger books support more photos per page without the images feeling miniaturized. A 12x12 book can comfortably hold 4-6 photos on a spread and still give each one room to breathe. An 8x8 book with the same number of images per spread will make each photo too small to appreciate.

As a general rule:

  • 8x8: 1-2 photos per page
  • 10x10: 1-4 photos per page
  • 12x12: 1-6 photos per page

White space

Smaller books require more discipline with white space. In an 8x8, a single photo with wide margins looks refined. Two photos with the same margins start feeling tight. In a 12x12, you have room for generous margins even with multiple images.

Text and captions

If your book is text-heavy — journal entries, extended captions, poems, letters — go larger. Text alongside photos needs breathing room, and a small page with both text and images quickly feels cluttered. A 10x10 or larger gives you space to pair a photo with a meaningful caption without one crowding the other.

How size affects price

Photo book pricing typically scales with surface area. A 12x12 book has 2.25 times the surface area of an 8x8 book, and the price reflects that — though rarely at a 2.25x multiple, because base costs (binding, covers, setup) stay constant regardless of size.

General pricing tiers across most services:

  • Small (8x8, 6x6): Most affordable. Good for projects where budget is the primary constraint.
  • Medium (10x10, 8x10, 11x8.5): Moderate price increase. The best value per square inch of photo space for most people.
  • Large (11x14, 12x12, 13x10): Premium pricing. Reserve these for projects where size is a priority — coffee table displays, portfolios, or once-in-a-lifetime events.

Shipping costs also increase with size and weight. A 12x12 hardcover book weighs significantly more than an 8x8, which matters if you're ordering multiple copies or shipping internationally.

Choosing based on your photos

Your photos should drive the decision more than anything else. Here's a quick decision framework:

  1. Check your photo orientation. Open your photo collection and roughly count: are most images landscape (horizontal), portrait (vertical), or a mix? If 70%+ lean one direction, match the book format to that orientation. If it's an even split, go square.
  2. Count your photos. If you have 30-50 images, a smaller book (8x8 or 8x10) will feel complete. If you have 100-200 images, you need a medium or large format to give each photo adequate space without running to 100+ pages.
  3. Consider the viewing context. Will this book sit on a coffee table for guests to browse? Go 10x10 or larger. Will it live on a bookshelf and come out occasionally? 8x10 or 10x10 works fine. Is it a gift to be mailed? 8x8 keeps shipping reasonable.
  4. Think about the occasion. A wedding album or milestone anniversary deserves large format. A summer vacation recap or birthday party book works perfectly at medium size. A quick holiday gift for relatives is ideal at small size.

Page count considerations

Size and page count work together. A thick small book and a thin large book can hold the same number of photos, but they feel very different.

  • 20-30 pages: Best in medium or large formats. A thin book in a small format feels insubstantial. At 11x14, 20 pages feels like a curated gallery. At 8x8, it feels like you ran out of photos.
  • 40-60 pages: The sweet spot for any size. This is long enough to tell a complete story and short enough to feel like an intentional edit, not an exhaustive archive.
  • 80-100+ pages: Best in small or medium formats. An 80-page book at 12x12 becomes heavy and awkward. The same page count at 8x10 feels like a satisfying novel-length experience.

Most services have minimum and maximum page counts. Typical minimums are 20-24 pages, and maximums range from 100 to 400+ pages depending on the binding type. If you find yourself hitting the maximum, consider splitting the project into two volumes rather than cramming everything into one oversized book.

Display and storage

One last practical consideration: where will this book live?

  • Coffee table: 10x10 or larger. The book needs to be proportional to the table surface and visible enough that guests notice it and pick it up. A small book on a large coffee table disappears.
  • Bookshelf: Any size works, but 8x10 and 10x10 integrate best with standard bookshelves. Larger formats may need to lie flat rather than stand upright, taking more shelf depth.
  • Nightstand or end table: 8x8 or 8x10. Something compact that can be picked up and browsed before bed.
  • Gift for mailing: 8x8 is the most practical for shipping. It fits in a standard padded mailer and shipping costs stay reasonable.

If you plan to make a series — an annual family yearbook, for example — pick one size and stick with it. A matching set of books on a shelf looks intentional and beautiful. A collection of mismatched sizes looks disorganized.
