7 Photo Book Styles: Find the Right Look for Your Album

November 2026 · 10 min read
Written by Olga Miller, founder of ReMeet

The seven main photo book styles are minimalist, magazine, scrapbook, classic, modern, editorial, and documentary. The right one depends on the occasion and the photos you have. ReMeet designers choose the style that fits your specific photos - no templates, no guessing.

Take the same 200 photos and design two different books. One with clean white backgrounds and a single image per page. The other with layered collages, playful borders, and handwritten captions. Same photos. Completely different feeling.

The style of your photo book shapes how people experience your memories. It sets the tone before anyone reads a caption or recognizes a face. A minimalist layout says something different about a wedding than a scrapbook layout does - even if the photos inside are identical.

This guide walks through seven distinct photo book styles. Each one has a look, a feeling, and a set of occasions where it works best. By the end, you'll know which style fits your photos and more importantly, which one fits the story you want to tell.

1. Minimalist

Minimalist photo books are defined by what they leave out. White space dominates. Pages are uncluttered. Every element has a clear purpose.

In a minimalist book, you'll typically see one or two photos per page, surrounded by generous margins. Backgrounds are white or soft neutral tones. If captions appear at all, they're short - a date, a location, a few words. Typography is clean and understated, usually a thin sans-serif in a small point size.

The effect is gallery-like. Each photo gets its own moment. The reader's eye isn't pulled in multiple directions. It lands on the image, absorbs it, and moves on.

Best for: Fine art photography, newborn and baby photo books, couple portraits, any collection where individual images are strong enough to stand alone. If your photos were taken by a professional photographer, minimalist framing lets that quality shine without competing elements.

Watch out for: If you have hundreds of photos you want to include, minimalist style will produce a very thick (and expensive) book. This style rewards aggressive photo curation - fewer photos, more impact.

2. Magazine

Magazine-style books borrow directly from editorial print design. Full-bleed images run to the edge of the page. Text overlays sit on top of photos. Headlines and pull quotes break up visual sections.

The layouts feel intentional and polished, like opening a travel feature in a glossy publication. Spreads mix large hero images with smaller supporting shots. Some pages dedicate space to text blocks that read like magazine articles - a narrative paragraph about the trip, the event, the day.

Color grading tends to be consistent and deliberate. Photos are edited to feel cohesive, as if they were shot for the same editorial assignment. Bold sans-serif fonts, uppercase headers, and thin rule lines add to the editorial atmosphere.

Best for: Travel books, coffee table photo books, fashion or lifestyle shoots, any project where you want the book to feel like a publication rather than a personal album. It works especially well when you have a mix of wide landscape shots and tight detail images.

Watch out for: Magazine style requires strong typography and careful text placement. If text overlays land on busy areas of a photo, the whole page falls apart. This style takes more design skill than it appears.

3. Scrapbook

Scrapbook style is the most playful and personal of the seven. Photos appear at angles. Borders, frames, and decorative elements surround images. Handwritten-style fonts add captions and notes. Some designs include digital stickers, tape effects, or patterned backgrounds.

The look is layered and warm. Pages feel like someone sat down with scissors, glue, and a stack of prints - but with the precision that only digital design allows. Colors tend toward soft pastels or warm earth tones, though bright and colorful palettes work for children's books.

Scrapbook style embraces imperfection. Photos don't need to be perfectly composed or professionally lit. Candid moments, blurry action shots, and funny outtakes feel right at home in a scrapbook layout.

Best for: Baby's first year, kids' birthday parties, school memories, family vacations, any occasion that's more about joy and spontaneity than formal elegance. It's also the style that best accommodates memorabilia - ticket stubs, handwritten notes, children's drawings scanned and placed alongside photos.

Watch out for: Scrapbook layouts can tip from charming to chaotic quickly. The key is using decorative elements consistently. Pick a color palette and a limited set of embellishments, then repeat them throughout. Too many different frames, stickers, and fonts on the same page creates visual noise.

4. Classic

Classic style is what most people picture when they think "photo album." Photos are centered on the page. Backgrounds are cream, ivory, or soft gray. Thin borders frame each image. The overall feeling is formal, timeless, and refined.

Layouts follow symmetrical patterns. If there are two photos on a spread, they're usually the same size, evenly placed. Text appears in elegant serif fonts - think names and dates rather than long narratives. Chapter dividers may feature ornamental details or a simple monogram.

Classic books don't try to be trendy. That's their strength. The design conventions come from decades of fine bookmaking and traditional album craftsmanship. A classic-style book made today will look just as appropriate in twenty years.

Best for: Weddings, formal portraits, milestone anniversaries, memorial or tribute books, heritage projects. If the book will sit on a shelf for generations, classic style ages gracefully. It's the safest choice when you're making a photo book as a gift and aren't sure of the recipient's taste.

Watch out for: Classic layouts can feel stiff if the photos themselves are casual. A centered, cream-bordered layout works beautifully for a posed family portrait but feels oddly formal around a candid beach snapshot. Match the formality of the style to the formality of the photos.

5. Modern

Modern style breaks the rules of classic design on purpose. Layouts are asymmetric. Photos are cropped in unexpected ways. Bold typography - oversized, colorful, or placed at angles - becomes a design element as prominent as the photos themselves.

Grids mix photo sizes freely. A large portrait might sit next to two tiny squares and a wide horizontal strip, all on the same page. Negative space is used dramatically - a small photo in the corner of an otherwise empty page. Colors can be bolder: black backgrounds, neon accents, or duotone effects on selected images.

The overall impression is fresh, energetic, and design-forward. Modern books feel like they were made by someone who pays attention to visual culture - Instagram, design magazines, contemporary art.

Best for: Travel adventures, city trips, creative portfolios, group friend trips, any occasion with energy and variety. Modern style works well when you have a wide range of photo types - landscapes, food, architecture, people - because the mixed grid format naturally accommodates different orientations and subjects.

Watch out for: Asymmetric layouts require a good sense of visual balance. What looks intentionally off-center to a designer can look like a mistake to everyone else. If you're designing this yourself, study modern layout examples first. Or let a designer handle the composition.

6. Editorial

Editorial style is story-driven above all else. Where other styles prioritize visual impact, editorial style prioritizes narrative. The book reads like a long-form feature - chapters have titles, spreads have opening paragraphs, and the text isn't just captions but actual storytelling.

Photos serve the narrative rather than the other way around. A spread might open with a half-page of text introducing a chapter ("The morning of the ceremony"), followed by images sequenced to unfold that chapter's story. Pull quotes highlight key moments. Some pages are entirely text.

The design language is sophisticated but restrained. Serif fonts dominate. Layouts have clear hierarchy - you always know what to look at first. The book has a beginning, middle, and end that you can feel as you turn the pages.

Best for: Wedding stories, family histories, retirement tributes, any project where the words matter as much as the images. Editorial style is ideal when you have stories to tell alongside the photos - anecdotes, timelines, letters, or quotes from the people in the pictures.

Watch out for: This style requires writing. Good writing. If the text reads like placeholder copy, it undermines the entire approach. Only choose editorial if you (or someone you trust) can write the narrative portions with genuine care.

7. Documentary

Documentary style follows the story as it happened. Photos appear in chronological order. Candid shots take priority over posed ones. The design stays out of the way - simple layouts, minimal decoration, and just enough text to anchor the time and place.

Think of it as photojournalism in book form. The morning coffee. The car packed with luggage. The first glimpse of the destination. The late-night conversations. Every moment gets equal treatment, not just the highlights.

Layouts tend to be straightforward - photos placed cleanly on white or off-white pages with simple date stamps or location markers. What makes documentary style compelling isn't the design but the completeness of the record. The book becomes an honest account of how things actually looked and felt.

Best for: Travel journals, day-in-the-life projects, year-in-review books, pregnancy and newborn documentation, any occasion where the sequence of events tells a story on its own. Documentary style is especially powerful for everyday moments that might otherwise be forgotten - the messy kitchen, the rainy afternoon, the unremarkable Tuesday that turned out to matter.

Watch out for: Without some curation, documentary books can feel like a photo dump. Even in a chronological, candid-first approach, you still need to select your strongest images and leave out the near-duplicates. The goal is an honest record, not an exhaustive one.

How to choose your style

The best style depends on two things: the occasion and the photos you have.

Here's a quick guide:

  • Wedding - Classic or editorial. Classic for timeless elegance, editorial if you want to tell the full story of the day with narrative text alongside the images.
  • Travel - Modern or documentary. Modern if the trip was visually diverse and energetic, documentary if you want a chronological journal of the journey.
  • Baby's first year - Minimalist or scrapbook. Minimalist for a clean, gallery-like showcase of professional photos, scrapbook for a warm and playful mix of everyday moments.
  • Family yearbook - Documentary or modern. Documentary to capture the year as it happened, modern for a design-forward collection of the highlights.
  • Coffee table book - Magazine or minimalist. Magazine for a glossy, editorial presentation, minimalist for fine art photography that speaks for itself.
  • Memorial or tribute - Classic or editorial. Classic for formal elegance, editorial for a richer narrative with stories and quotes.
  • Gift for grandparents - Classic or scrapbook. Classic for something they'll display proudly, scrapbook for something grandchildren helped "make."

If you're still unsure, ask yourself: when someone opens this book, what should they feel first? If the answer is calm and awe, go minimalist. If it's warmth and fun, go scrapbook. If it's "I can't put this down," go editorial or documentary.
