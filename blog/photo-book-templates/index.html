Photo Book Templates: Layouts for Every Occasion

Written by Olga Miller, founder of ReMeet

Every photo book service offers templates: pre-built page layouts where you drop in your photos. Some services have dozens, others have hundreds. But templates are not all created equal, and choosing the wrong one can make your book look worse than no template at all.

This guide covers the main types of photo book templates, when each one works, and when you're better off skipping templates entirely.

The four main template types

1. Grid layouts

The most common template: a page divided into equal rectangles. 2x2, 3x3, 2x3. Every photo gets the same size and shape.

Works well for: School photos, team pictures, any collection where consistency matters more than drama.

The problem: Grids treat every photo the same. Your best photo gets the same small rectangle as a mediocre snapshot. No visual hierarchy. After 10 pages of identical grids, the book feels monotonous.

2. Collage layouts

Photos of different sizes arranged at various positions. Some overlap. Some have borders, others bleed to the edge. The effect is dynamic.

Works well for: Event recaps, birthday parties, group trips with lots of candid shots.

The problem: Pre-built collages have fixed slots. If the template expects a landscape photo but your best shot is portrait, you're forced to crop it awkwardly or swap in a worse photo that fits.

3. Timeline / chronological layouts

Pages with date markers, chapter headings, and a clear before-to-after flow. Some include month labels or milestone flags.

Works well for: Baby books, yearbooks, school memory books.

The problem: Real life doesn't divide evenly. January might have 50 photos, February might have 5. Fixed timeline templates give every month the same pages.

4. Full-bleed / magazine layouts

Photos extending to the page edge, sometimes spanning two pages. Minimal borders. Bold and cinematic.

Works well for: Travel books, weddings, coffee table books.

The problem: Requires high-quality images. A low-resolution phone photo stretched full-page looks pixelated. Auto-cropping can cut off faces.

The template trap

Templates create an illusion of simplicity. You see a beautiful example on the service's website and think your book will look the same. But the example was made by a designer using photos that fit the template perfectly.

When you fill it with your actual photos:

  • Cropping issues. The template expects specific aspect ratios. Your photos don't match. Automatic cropping cuts off faces or leaves awkward space.
  • Quality mismatch. Some photos are sharp, others were taken in low light. A template treats them all the same.
  • Repetitive layouts. Most people pick one theme and stick with it. 30 pages of the same layout.
  • Time investment. Choosing a template: 20 minutes. Filling it: 2 to 4 hours. Fixing issues: another hour. Total: 3 to 5 hours.

When templates work

  • Small, simple projects. A 20-page book with 30 photos.
  • You enjoy the process. If you like arranging photos, templates give you creative control.
  • Consistent photo quality. Professional portraits all in the same style.
  • Budget is the only priority. Shutterfly templates start at $10 during sales.

When custom design is better

  • Large photo collections. 200 to 5,000 photos need curation, not just layout.
  • Mixed quality. A designer knows to give great shots full-page spreads and use good-but-not-great shots as smaller supporting images.
  • Varied layouts. Custom design varies page by page. The book has rhythm instead of repetition.
  • Proper cropping. Every photo is cropped individually to protect faces and preserve context.
  • Time matters. A done-for-you service saves the time entirely.
