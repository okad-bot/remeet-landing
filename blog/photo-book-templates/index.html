Photo Book Templates: Ready-Made Page Plans by Occasion

November 2026 · 10 min read
Written by Olga Miller, founder of ReMeet

Good photo book templates use consistent spacing, varied layouts, and room for captions. This guide covers templates for baby, wedding, travel, family, and gift books. If you would rather skip templates entirely, ReMeet designs every page from scratch around your photos. From $29.

A photo book template is a starting point, not a prison. It gives you a page plan: how many photos per spread, where text goes, what size each image should be. The value of a good template is that it removes the blank-page problem. Instead of staring at an empty canvas wondering where to put your first photo, you drop images into a structure someone already thought through.

But templates only work when they match the story you're telling. A wedding book needs different page plans than a travel journal. A baby's first year demands a different rhythm than a family yearbook. This guide walks through the best photo book templates for each occasion, explains what makes them work, and shows you where templates stop being helpful.

What makes a good photo book template

Before diving into occasion-specific templates, it's worth understanding what separates a well-designed template from a mediocre one. Three things matter most:

Consistent spacing. The margins between photos, between photos and the page edge, and between photos and text should follow a clear system. If the gap between two photos is 8mm on one page and 14mm on the next, the book feels sloppy even if the photos are beautiful. Good templates lock these values down so every page feels like it belongs to the same book.

Varied layouts. A template that uses the same two-photo grid on every page produces a monotonous book. The best templates alternate between full-bleed single images, multi-photo spreads, and text pages. This variation creates rhythm. The reader's eye stays engaged because each turn of the page offers something visually different. For more on layout principles, see our photo book layout tips guide.

Room for captions. Even if you don't plan to add text, a good template reserves space for it. Dates, locations, names, short stories. These details become more valuable over time. A template that leaves zero text space forces you to overlay words on photos, which almost always looks worse than text in its own designated area.

Baby photo book templates

A baby photo book is usually the first photo book parents ever make, and it's the one most likely to stall halfway through. Templates help here because the structure is so natural: babies change fast, and the book follows that change.

Monthly milestones template. The most popular baby book template dedicates one spread to each month. The left page holds one large portrait (the classic monthly photo, same chair or blanket each time), and the right page holds 3-4 smaller candid shots from that month plus a text block for milestones: first smile, first tooth, first crawl. Twelve months, twelve spreads, plus a cover and a closing page. Clean and complete.

First-year timeline template. Instead of one spread per month, this template runs chronologically with more flexibility. Some months get two spreads (the newborn phase, the holidays), others get one. The timeline runs along the bottom of each page, marking dates. This format works better when you have an uneven number of photos across months.

Growth chart page. A single dedicated page that shows the baby at 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months side by side. Same framing, same background, different tiny human. This page alone justifies making the book. It's the spread parents come back to most often.

Wedding photo book templates

A wedding photo book follows the day's natural arc, which makes the template structure nearly universal: getting ready, ceremony, portraits, reception, dancing, departure. The details vary, but the flow is the same.

Ceremony-to-reception flow. The standard wedding template moves through the day chronologically. Getting-ready pages use intimate detail shots (dress, rings, shoes) alongside candid portraits. The ceremony section opens with a full-bleed establishing shot of the venue, then alternates between wide ceremony moments and close-up reactions. The reception section picks up energy: toasts, first dance, cake, candid crowd shots.

Detail pages. Good wedding templates include dedicated detail spreads between the major sections. A spread of just the invitation suite, table settings, flowers, and signage. These pages serve as visual breathers. They slow the reader down between the emotion-heavy ceremony and reception sections.

Full-bleed ceremony shots. At least two or three spreads in a wedding template should be reserved for full-bleed images that span both pages. The first kiss, the recessional walk, the wide reception hall shot. These are the pages that make the book feel cinematic rather than like a photo gallery. The rest of the template should complement these hero spreads, not compete with them.

Wedding books typically run 40-60 pages. A template shorter than 40 pages will feel rushed; one longer than 60 risks repetition. For ideas on choosing the right visual approach, our photo book styles guide covers layout and design aesthetics in detail.

Travel photo book templates

Travel books have a unique challenge: the photos span landscapes, food, architecture, people, and random moments, all mixed together. A good travel photo book template imposes just enough structure to hold this variety together.

Map page. The best travel templates start with a map. It can be simple: a map of the country or region with dots marking the places you visited. This single page gives the reader context for everything that follows. It also solves the "where was this?" problem years later when you've forgotten the name of that village in Tuscany.

Day-by-day chapters. Organizing a travel book by day is the most intuitive approach, and templates that use this structure work well. Each chapter gets a title page (Day 3: Kyoto) followed by 2-4 spreads of that day's photos. This prevents the common problem where all the photos blur together into one undifferentiated stream of vacation shots.

Food, landscape, and people mix. Within each chapter, a strong travel template alternates between photo types. A wide landscape spread, then a grid of food close-ups, then a portrait page. This variety keeps the book visually interesting and captures the full texture of the trip, not just the scenery.

One useful travel template trick: end the book with a favorites page. One photo from each day, lined up. It gives the book a satisfying sense of closure.

Family yearbook templates

A family yearbook captures a full year for your household. It's the broadest type of photo book, covering everything from birthdays and holidays to regular Tuesday evenings. Templates for yearbooks need to accommodate this range without feeling chaotic.

Seasonal chapters. The most effective family yearbook template divides the year into four sections: winter, spring, summer, fall. Each chapter opens with a full-page seasonal photo (bare trees, blooming flowers, beach day, leaves changing) and a simple chapter title. Within each section, pages flow chronologically through that quarter's events.

Stats page. A single spread near the front or back of the book that captures the family's year in numbers: ages, grades, heights, books read, places visited, meals cooked, movies watched. This page is completely text-based, and it becomes one of the most treasured pages in the book as years pass. Many yearbook templates include this, and if yours doesn't, add it manually.

Family portrait spread. One formal (or deliberately informal) family photo, printed large. Every yearbook needs this. It doesn't have to be a professional studio shot. A self-timer photo in the living room works perfectly. The point is the record: here we are, all of us, this year.

Yearbooks work best at 40-60 pages. That's roughly 10-15 pages per season, which is enough to include the major moments without turning the book into an exhaustive archive.

Gift and occasion templates

Not every photo book is a comprehensive record. Some are gifts: a birthday book for a friend, a grandparents' brag book, a memorial for someone you've lost. These books are shorter, typically 20-30 pages, and the templates reflect that.

Birthday book template. A simple structure: a dedication page, a spread for each year of friendship or each major birthday, and a closing message. The focus is on curated highlights, not comprehensive coverage. Five great photos are better than fifty average ones.

Grandparents book template. Photos of the grandchildren, organized by child or by event. Big photos, minimal text, generous print size. Remember that grandparents' books often sit on a coffee table and get shown to visitors, so the layout should be clear and the photos should be printed large enough to see without reading glasses.

Memorial book template. These books move through a life: childhood, young adulthood, family years, later years. The template is chronological and gentle. Generous white space, muted backgrounds, room for quotes or memories from family members. Memorial templates typically run 20-30 pages and prioritize dignity over completeness.

For all gift books, the template should include a dedication page at the front. Even a single sentence ("For Grandma, with love from the whole family, Christmas 2026") transforms a photo book from a product into a gift.

The template trap: when templates limit you

Templates are helpful until they're not. Here's when they start working against you:

Forced grids. Some templates lock you into rigid grids: four photos per page, every page, no exceptions. This ignores the fact that some moments deserve a full page while others work best small. If a template doesn't let you vary the number of photos per spread, it's too rigid.

Identical pages. When you flip through a finished book and every spread looks the same, the template has failed. The hallmark of good design is variation within consistency. Same margins and fonts, yes. Same layout on every page, no.

No variation in pacing. A book needs fast pages and slow pages. A spread with six small photos moves quickly. A single full-bleed image asks the reader to pause. Templates that use the same density on every page create a monotonous rhythm, like a song played at one volume.

Mismatch with your photos. This is the most common issue. You pick a template designed for a wedding, but your wedding photos are mostly candid and the template expects formal portraits. Or you choose a travel template designed for landscape-heavy trips, but your photos are mostly street photography and food. When the template and the photos don't match, one of them has to bend, and it shouldn't be the photos.

The core problem with templates is that they're designed before your photos exist. They're built for a hypothetical set of images, not yours. And that gap between the template's assumptions and your actual photos is where most photo book frustration lives.
