Photo book templates: why you might not need one

November 2026 · 7 min read
Written by Olga Miller, founder of ReMeet

Every DIY photo book editor starts the same way: pick a template. Templates are pre-designed page layouts with placeholder slots for your photos. They're supposed to make the process easier. Sometimes they do. Sometimes they create more problems than they solve.

What templates actually do

A template is a fixed grid of photo slots with predefined sizes, positions, and text areas. You drop your photos into the slots. The editor crops them to fit. Backgrounds, colors, and fonts come pre-set.

Think of it like a form you fill in. The layout is already decided. Your job is to provide the content. This works well when your content happens to match the form. It works poorly when it doesn't.

The good

Templates have genuine advantages, especially for simple projects.

  • Fast start. No blank page anxiety. You open the editor and there's already a layout waiting. Just add photos.
  • Consistent look. Every page follows the same design system. Fonts match, spacing is even, colors coordinate. You don't need design skills to get a coherent result.
  • Works for simple projects. Twenty photos from one event, all in the same orientation? A template handles that perfectly. Pick a grid, drop in the photos, order the book.
  • Free in most editors. Shutterfly, Mixbook, Canva, and Google Photos all include templates at no extra cost. You're paying only for printing.

The not-so-good

The problems with templates show up the moment your photos don't fit the mold.

Your photos get cropped to fit the template, not the other way around. A template slot is a fixed rectangle. If your photo is a different aspect ratio, the editor crops it automatically. That means heads get cut off, important details at the edges disappear, and the composition the photographer intended gets lost. You can manually adjust the crop for each photo, but multiply that by 60 pages and you've just added an hour to your project.

Every page looks the same. Templates repeat the same layout across pages. Two photos per page, three photos per page, four photos per page. After ten pages, the repetitive grid feeling is hard to ignore. Real books, the kind you see on a coffee table or in a bookstore, vary their layouts from spread to spread. Templates don't.

Photo count per page is fixed. What if you have three photos for a page that holds four? You either leave an empty slot (which looks unfinished) or add a filler photo (which dilutes the page). What if you have one stunning photo that deserves a full page? Most templates don't offer a single-image option in the same theme.

Text areas are fixed size. Your caption either doesn't fit or looks tiny in an oversized box. There's no middle ground. The template decides where text goes and how much space it gets, regardless of what you actually want to say.

Switching templates means starting over. If you're 30 pages into a project and realize the template isn't working, changing it resets all your photo placements. Every crop adjustment, every photo swap, every text edit is gone. Most people won't start over at that point. They'll settle for a book they're not happy with.

Template-based services compared

Not all template editors are created equal. Here's how the major services compare.

Shutterfly has the most templates of any service, covering every theme from baby books to travel journals. The editor is functional but dated. Quality is basic: thin paper, standard printing. Templates are rigid and hard to customize beyond swapping photos.

Mixbook has the best template editor. You can resize and move photo slots, change backgrounds per page, and adjust text freely. It's the closest a template-based service gets to custom design. The AI Story Mode can generate an initial layout, though you'll still want to review and adjust every page.

Canva offers free photo book templates with a design-focused interface. The templates are stylish, but Canva isn't built specifically for photo books. Page sizes are limited, print integration is handled through third parties, and the templates prioritize graphic design over photo display.

Google Photos is the simplest option. It auto-fills a book from your library with minimal templates. Very few layout choices, very few customization options, but the lowest effort required. Good for a fast, no-frills book when you just want something printed.

When a template is fine

Templates aren't always the wrong choice. They work well in specific situations.

  • Small projects under 20 pages. With fewer photos, the repetitive-grid problem is less noticeable, and the time investment is small.
  • When you enjoy designing. If arranging photos on a page is fun for you, not a chore, templates give you a starting point to build from.
  • Uniform photos. If all your photos are the same orientation, similar content, and roughly the same quality, they'll fit neatly into template slots without awkward cropping.
  • Budget under $15. When cost is the primary constraint, a template-based softcover from Shutterfly or Snapfish gets the job done for the lowest price.

When custom design wins

Custom design becomes the better choice as projects get bigger and more meaningful.

  • Large photo collections. When you have 100+ photos to organize, sorting and placing them into template slots takes hours. The bigger the project, the more time custom design saves you.
  • Mixed orientations. Real photo collections include vertical, horizontal, and square images. Templates force them all into the same slots. Custom layouts adapt to each photo.
  • Meaningful projects. Weddings, a baby's first year, memorial books. These are books you'll keep for decades. The difference between a template grid and a designed layout is the difference between a printed photo dump and a book that tells a story.
  • When you want it to look like a real book. Open any professionally published photo book and you'll see varied layouts, intentional white space, and photos sized to match their importance. That's what custom design delivers. Templates deliver a grid.
  • When you don't have time to design. This is the most common reason. You have the photos. You want the book. You don't have a free afternoon to sit in an editor dragging photos into slots.
