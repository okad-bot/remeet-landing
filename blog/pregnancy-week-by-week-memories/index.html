Pregnancy Week by Week: What to Document and Remember

September 2026 · 15 min read

Pregnancy is one of the most documented experiences in human history and simultaneously one of the most forgotten. You'll remember that you were exhausted in the first trimester and uncomfortable in the third. But the details, the specific cravings, the exact moment you felt the first kick, the ridiculous nickname you gave the baby before you knew their name, those evaporate faster than you'd expect.

Your brain is literally rewiring during pregnancy. The hormones that help you bond with your baby also helpfully erase the uncomfortable specifics of the experience. It's evolutionary. If women remembered exactly how pregnancy felt, the species might have a population problem.

This guide is about capturing the details while you're living them. Not for Instagram, not for a perfectly curated aesthetic. For you. For your future self who will want to remember what this was actually like. And someday, maybe, for your kid, who will want to know the story of how they got here.

First trimester: The secret (weeks 1-12)

For most people, the first trimester is the most surreal part of pregnancy. You are growing a human being, and almost nobody knows about it. You look the same. You go to work. You attend dinners and decline wine with increasingly elaborate excuses ("I'm on antibiotics" works once; "I'm doing a cleanse" buys you another week). Meanwhile, your body is doing something extraordinary and making you feel terrible as a thank-you.

What's actually happening

The shock. Even if you planned this pregnancy, even if you tried for months, the positive test is still a jolt. There's a gap between intellectually wanting a baby and emotionally processing the fact that one is coming. Some people feel immediate joy. Some feel terror. Some feel nothing and then feel guilty about feeling nothing. All of these are valid first reactions.

Symptoms nobody warned you about. Morning sickness gets all the press, but first trimester symptoms are a whole spectrum. There's the exhaustion that makes you want to sleep at your desk at 2pm. The heightened sense of smell that turns your partner's perfectly normal deodorant into a chemical weapon. The bloating that makes you look four months pregnant before you're even three months. The food aversions that turn your favorite foods into enemies. And the anxiety, the constant, low-level worry that something might go wrong, because it's too early to feel the baby move and too early to tell most people, so you're sitting with this enormous secret and just... hoping.

The first ultrasound. Depending on your doctor and your circumstances, this might happen around 8-10 weeks. Hearing the heartbeat for the first time is one of those moments that no amount of preparation can match. Some people cry. Some laugh. Some stare at the screen trying to figure out which blob is the baby (don't worry, the technician will point). If you can, record the audio of the heartbeat. It's the first sound your baby ever makes, and you'll want to hear it again.

Telling people. The "12-week rule," waiting until the end of the first trimester to announce, exists because miscarriage risk drops significantly after this point. But it's a guideline, not a law. Some people tell close family right away because they'd want support regardless of outcome. Some people wait even longer. There's no wrong approach. The announcement itself, whether it's a phone call to your mom, a group text, or an elaborate social media reveal, is worth documenting. Your parents' reaction when they found out they were going to be grandparents? That's a memory worth keeping.

Second trimester: The golden period (weeks 13-27)

There's a reason people call this the "golden trimester." The nausea usually fades. The energy comes back. You're visibly pregnant now, which means people offer you seats and ask questions and occasionally try to touch your belly (it's fine to not be fine with this). You feel, for the first time in months, almost like yourself again. Except you're building a person.

What's happening

Finding out the gender (or not). Around 18-20 weeks, the anatomy scan reveals whether your baby has the parts typically associated with one sex or another. Some parents want to know immediately. Some want to be surprised. Some find out and keep it a secret from everyone else. Some do the whole "gender reveal" thing (no pyrotechnics, please). Whatever you choose, document the moment. The envelope from the doctor. The phone call to your partner. The reaction when you told people, or didn't.

The quickening. The first time you feel the baby move. Early on, it feels like bubbles or butterflies, and you'll spend a week wondering if it's the baby or just gas. (It's often gas. But eventually it's definitely the baby.) By the end of the second trimester, those flutters become unmistakable kicks and rolls, and your partner can feel them too. The first time someone else feels the baby kick is a moment worth writing down. What were you doing? Where were you? What was their reaction?

Nursery planning. This is when the nesting instinct starts to kick in. Paint colors, crib selection, the great "should we buy a changing table or just use the dresser" debate. It's both exciting and overwhelming. The nursery in progress, half-painted walls, unopened boxes of tiny clothes, the crib still in its packaging, makes for surprisingly emotional photos later.

The baby shower. Whether it's a traditional shower, a co-ed party, a virtual celebration, or a casual brunch, document it. The decorations, the cake, the onesies people bought, the unsolicited advice from every parent in the room. And write down the funny stuff: the gag gifts, the horror stories people think are appropriate to share with a pregnant woman, the look on your partner's face during the diaper cake assembly.

This trimester is the sweet spot for a professional maternity photoshoot if that's something you want. But even if it's not, take regular bump photos. Same spot, same angle, every week or two. The progression is astonishing when you see it compiled in a pregnancy photo book. Your belly at 14 weeks versus 26 weeks tells a visual story that you'll find hard to believe later.

Third trimester: The home stretch (weeks 28-40+)

The final act. Your baby is viable outside the womb now, which is both reassuring and terrifying. You're getting bigger by the day, and basic activities like tying your shoes, getting off the couch, and sleeping become genuinely challenging feats of engineering.

What's happening

Nesting goes into overdrive. You might find yourself organizing the closet at midnight, deep-cleaning the bathroom grout, or alphabetizing the spice rack. This is hormonal and primal and completely normal. Document the nesting, photos of the organized drawers, the meal prep freezer filled with labeled containers, the obsessively folded baby clothes. You did that. It matters.

The hospital bag. Packing this is a rite of passage. You'll read seventeen different checklists online and still forget something. What goes in the bag is less important than the act of packing it, the moment it becomes real that you're going to a hospital (or a birth center, or setting up the living room) and coming home with a baby. Take a photo of the packed bag. It represents the last moment of "before."

The birth plan. Write one if it helps you feel prepared, but hold it loosely. Birth plans are wish lists, not contracts. The baby hasn't read yours and doesn't care about your preferences for ambient lighting. What matters is that you feel informed about your options and that your medical team knows your priorities. Document your hopes and your fears. Write them down, not for the medical staff, but for yourself. Future you will want to remember what you were thinking in those final weeks.

Last date nights. The last dinner out as a couple before becoming parents. The last lazy Saturday morning. The last spontaneous anything. These "lasts" feel both mundane and monumental. You probably won't recognize them as lasts until they've already happened. But if you do, take a photo. Write a note.

Letters to your baby. This one surprises a lot of people with how emotional it gets. Sit down and write a letter to the baby who hasn't arrived yet. What are you hoping for them? What are you afraid of? What does your life look like right now, the life they're about to turn upside down? This letter is one of the most powerful things you can include in a baby book. Imagine reading it when they're 18.

What to photograph each trimester

First trimester

  • The positive pregnancy test (the original, not a staged one)
  • Your face when you found out, if someone was there to capture it
  • The first ultrasound printout
  • Your "flat" belly before it all begins
  • Symptoms in action: the saltines on the nightstand, the pregnancy pillow you bought at week 8
  • Telling your partner, parents, best friend (video if possible)

Second trimester

  • Weekly or bi-weekly bump progression (same spot, same outfit works best)
  • Nursery in progress, the mess and the vision
  • Baby shower moments
  • Gender reveal reaction (if applicable)
  • The first pair of baby shoes you bought
  • Partner talking to or reading to the belly
  • Cravings in action (midnight ice cream runs count)

Third trimester

  • The full bump at its biggest
  • Finished nursery
  • Packed hospital bag
  • Last belly photo before delivery
  • Partner and belly together
  • Pets with the belly (they know something's up)
  • The calendar or countdown, however you're tracking
  • The "before" photo: the last photo of your family at its current size

What to write down (you'll forget more than you think)

Photos capture what things looked like. Writing captures what they felt like. Here's what's worth putting into words:

  • The cravings list. Every weird food combination, every 10pm request, every "I know this is strange but I need pickles and mango." You'll laugh at these later.
  • The baby's nickname. Almost every couple has one: Peanut, Bean, Nugget, The Alien. What did you call this baby before they had a name?
  • Funny moments. The time you cried at a dog food commercial. The time your partner panicked during a Braxton Hicks contraction. The baby's kicking pattern during every work meeting.
  • Fears and hopes. What keeps you up at night? What are you most excited about? These are raw and real and the kind of thing you'll want to remember you felt.
  • The name discussion. How you chose the name, what other names were in the running, the ones your partner vetoed and why.
  • What your life looks like right now. Where you live, what you do, what your daily routine is, what you eat for breakfast. These details feel mundane now and become fascinating context later.
  • Unsolicited advice. Keep a running list of the best (and worst) advice strangers gave you. It's comedy gold.

You don't need a special journal for this. A notes app on your phone works. Voice memos work even better since you can capture tone and emotion. The format doesn't matter. The habit of recording these details before they fade is what matters.

The partner's perspective: What to capture for them

Pregnancy documentation tends to focus on the pregnant person, which makes sense. But the partner's experience is also changing, and it's rarely documented.

Partners: Take photos of yourselves too. Write down how you're feeling. The excitement, the terror, the surreal moment you assembled the crib and realized you're going to be someone's parent. Your reaction to seeing the ultrasound. The first time you felt the baby kick through the belly. The thing you're most worried about. The thing you're most looking forward to.

Some partners write their own letters to the baby. If this feels right, do it. If it feels forced, a simple paragraph in your phone notes is enough. Something. Anything. Because your perspective is part of this story too.

For the pregnant partner: Take photos of your partner with the bump, not just staged ones, but the candid moments. Them assembling furniture, painting the nursery, falling asleep on the couch with their hand on your belly. These photos will matter to both of you and to your child eventually.

The waiting game: When baby doesn't come on time

Due dates are estimates. Only about 5% of babies arrive on their actual due date. Many first-time parents go past 40 weeks, and the waiting is a special kind of psychological torture.

Every day past the due date, you'll get approximately 47 texts asking "Any news??" Consider a preemptive group text: "We'll tell you when the baby comes. Until then, assume we're still waiting." This saves your sanity.

If you're induced or have a scheduled C-section, there's a strange calm to knowing the date. You go to bed as a couple (or a family of however many) and wake up knowing that by tonight, everything will be different. Document that morning. The drive to the hospital. The waiting room. The last "before" selfie. These are the final frames of one chapter and the first frames of the next.

When birth doesn't go as planned, and it often doesn't, the feelings can be complicated. Disappointment, relief, guilt, gratitude, trauma, all tangled together. These experiences are valid and important. If your birth story didn't match your birth plan, that's not a failure. It's reality. And it's still your story, worth telling and worth remembering, on your terms, when you're ready.

Keepsakes beyond photos

Not every pregnancy memory is a photograph. Some of the most meaningful keepsakes are things you might not think to save:

  • Ultrasound images. These fade on thermal paper. Scan or photograph them early so you don't lose them. The 3D ultrasound where the baby looks like a sleepy alien is oddly precious.
  • The belly cast. A plaster mold of your belly in the third trimester. It's messy to make and you'll wonder where to store it, but it captures the physical reality of pregnancy in a way photos can't.
  • A pregnancy journal or app entries. If you've been tracking in an app, export the data. Apps disappear. Screenshots last. Better yet, export and include the highlights in a printed book.
  • Cards and messages people sent. The congratulations cards, the texts from friends, the note from your grandmother. Photograph them or save them in a box.
  • The hospital bracelet. Tiny, flimsy, and surprisingly emotional.
  • The outfit you brought the baby home in. Save it. You'll hold it up years later and not believe a human was ever that small.

Building a pregnancy book: How and when

The ideal time to make a pregnancy photo book is during the third trimester, before the baby arrives. Here's why: once the baby is here, you will not have time, energy, or cognitive function to go back and organize nine months of photos and memories. You just won't. The project will sit in your "someday" folder alongside the baby book you were also going to fill out.

What to include

  • The announcement (test, ultrasound, however you shared it)
  • Bump progression photos
  • Key milestone moments: first kick, gender reveal, baby shower
  • Nursery transformation (before and after)
  • Letters, notes, journal entries (even just excerpts)
  • The funny stuff: cravings list, baby name candidates, unsolicited advice log
  • Partner's perspective and photos
  • The last belly photo
  • Bonus: leave a few blank pages at the end for birth day photos and a birth announcement

Structure that works

Organize by trimester, then by theme within each trimester. This gives you a natural three-act structure: the secret, the golden period, the home stretch. Each section can include photos, text, and keepsakes. It tells a complete story from "we found out" to "we're ready."
