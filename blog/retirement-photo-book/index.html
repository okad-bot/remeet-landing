Retirement Photo Book: A Group Gift from Colleagues

Someone on your team is retiring. You want to do something better than a card and a cake. A photo book with 10 or 20 years of team photos, messages from colleagues, and a few inside jokes is the kind of gift people actually keep.

The problem is that organizing it sounds like a project. Collecting photos from a dozen people, figuring out what goes where, and putting it all together on top of your actual job. That's where I come in. You collect the photos. I design the book.

How to organize a group retirement gift

One person takes the lead. That's you, if you're reading this. Here's the process:

  1. Create a shared album. A Google Photos shared album or a Dropbox folder works best. Name it something like "Photos for [Name]'s Book" so people know what it's for.
  2. Send the link to colleagues. A short email or Slack message is enough. "We're making a photo book for Sarah's retirement. Drop your favorite team photos here by [date]." Keep it simple.
  3. Set a deadline. Give people two weeks. Most will contribute in the first 48 hours after a reminder.
  4. Collect messages. Ask each person for one or two sentences: a favorite memory, an inside joke, or just "thanks for everything." These go into the book alongside the photos.
  5. Share everything with me. Send the album link when you place your order. I take it from there.

Your total time: about 30 minutes to set up the folder, write the message, and send one reminder. Everything else is on me.

What to include in a retirement photo book

The best retirement books mix the professional with the personal. Here's what I look for when designing:

  • Team events. Holiday parties, team lunches, offsite trips, award ceremonies. These are usually the easiest photos to find.
  • Office moments. Desk decorations, birthday cakes, the time someone brought their dog. The everyday stuff that makes a workplace feel like a community.
  • Milestones. Promotions, project launches, anniversary celebrations, conference presentations. The moments that mark a career.
  • The early years. If anyone has photos from the first day, the old office, or the original team, include them. The contrast between then and now is always a good page.
  • Funny moments. The Halloween costume, the questionable sweater, the team building exercise that went sideways. These are the pages that make people laugh.
  • Personal notes. Short messages from colleagues placed next to the photos. "Remember when we stayed until midnight to finish the launch." These turn a photo album into something with real feeling.

Don't worry about having the perfect mix. Send me everything you've got. I sort through it, remove duplicates, and build the story.

Collecting photos from a large group

Getting photos from 10 or 20 people sounds hard. It's actually straightforward if you set it up right.

Google Photos shared album is the easiest option. Create the album, enable link sharing, and send the link. Anyone with the link can add photos directly from their phone. No account needed, no app to install. I've had groups of 30 people use this without any issues.

Dropbox or Google Drive folder works well for people who have photos on their computer. Create a shared folder with upload permissions. Send the link. Done.

Tips that help:

  • Send the first message two to three weeks before you need the book.
  • Send one reminder after a week. That's when most photos come in.
  • Tell people there's no limit. Phone snapshots, event photos, screenshots of funny Slack messages. Everything counts.
  • Mention it's a surprise if it is. People are more motivated when they know it's a secret.

A group of 20 people typically contributes 200 to 500 photos. That's more than enough for a 30-page or even a 100-page book. I pick the best ones and organize them into chapters. For more on photo counts, see how many photos you need for a photo book.

Adding messages and quotes from the team

Written messages are what turn a retirement photo book from nice to meaningful. A photo of the team at a holiday party is good. That same photo with a note saying "The year we convinced Mark to wear a reindeer sweater, 2019" is better.

Here's what works:

  • Short and specific. "Thanks for mentoring me through my first big project" is better than "You were a great colleague."
  • One to three sentences. These get placed alongside photos, so they need to be short enough to fit on a page.
  • Handwritten is great. If someone writes their note by hand, scan it or take a photo. I place the handwritten version in the book next to the right photos.
  • Collect them separately. A shared Google Doc or a simple email thread works. I match each note to the person's photos.

Not everyone will write something, and that's fine. Even five or six personal notes spread across a 30-page book make a real difference.

Frequently asked questions

How do I collect photos from 20 people?

Create a shared Google Photos album or a Dropbox folder and send the link to everyone. Set a deadline two weeks before you need the book. Most people contribute within 48 hours of a reminder. You will usually get 200 to 500 photos from a group of 20, which is plenty for a 30 or 100 page book.

Can I include written messages from colleagues?

Yes. Ask each person to write a short note, a favorite memory, or a quote. Send them to me as a text file, a Google Doc, or individual messages. I place each note next to the matching photos in the book. Handwritten notes work too. Scan or photograph them and add them to the shared folder.

How fast can you make a retirement book?

First draft in 72 hours from when you share the photos. Each revision round takes about 24 hours. If you need it in a week, that is doable. The main bottleneck is usually collecting photos from colleagues, not the design itself.
