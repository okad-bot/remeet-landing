7 Shutterfly Alternatives in 2026 (Easier, Cheaper, or Both)

ReMeet is a done-for-you alternative to Shutterfly. Send a link to your photos and a designer builds the book from $79 for up to 100 pages: first proof in 72 hours, two rounds of edits, printed hardcover.

November 2026 · 10 min read

Shutterfly has been the default photo book choice for years. It's everywhere, it runs constant sales, and it offers everything from books to blankets to phone cases. But if you've actually used their photo book editor recently, you might have noticed it feels a little stuck in 2015.

The templates look dated. The editor is clunky. Cropping is hit-or-miss. And without a sale (which, to be fair, happens almost every week), pricing isn't great for what you get.

If you're exploring alternatives, here are seven services worth considering — each better than Shutterfly in at least one way. Some are cheaper. Some have better design tools. And one does the whole thing for you.

Now let's look at each one in detail.

1. Mixbook — Best editor, best templates

If your main complaint about Shutterfly is the design experience, Mixbook is the most direct upgrade. The drag-and-drop editor is genuinely intuitive. Templates are modern and well-designed. You get more creative freedom without feeling overwhelmed.

Mixbook also runs frequent sales — usually 40-50% off — which brings the price closer to Shutterfly's. Without a sale, 80-page hardcovers run $80-130, which is pricier. But the design quality of the final book is noticeably better.

Best for: People who want the DIY experience but with better tools and results than Shutterfly.

The catch: You're still designing every page yourself. Budget 2-4 hours for a good result. For a deeper comparison, see our Shutterfly vs Mixbook breakdown. If Mixbook isn't quite right either, we've also compared Mixbook alternatives.

2. Artifact Uprising — Premium quality, premium price

Artifact Uprising is what Shutterfly would be if it cared more about materials than volume. The paper is thick and luxurious. The covers feel substantial. The printing is consistently excellent. If you hold a Shutterfly book and an Artifact Uprising book side by side, the difference is obvious.

The editor is more of a blank canvas than a template system. That gives you full control, but it also means you need real design sense to make something that looks good. There's no "auto-fill" to fall back on.

Pricing reflects the quality: 80-page hardcovers start around $150 and can reach $220+ depending on size and paper type. Sales are rare and modest compared to Shutterfly's constant discounts.

Best for: People who enjoy designing and want a truly premium physical object — wedding books, milestone gifts, coffee-table keepsakes.

The catch: The most expensive option and the most time-intensive. A beautiful book if you finish it, but many projects stall because the editor demands more effort than people expect. See our Remeet vs Artifact Uprising comparison for a different approach to premium books.

3. Chatbooks — Fastest, simplest, cheapest

Chatbooks takes the opposite approach from Artifact Uprising. Instead of giving you maximum control, it gives you almost none — and that's the point. Connect your Instagram or camera roll, pick a date range, and the app generates a book automatically. You can swap out photos or reorder them, but there's no complex editor to learn.

Books are small (mostly 6x6 inches), softcover starts at $10-15, and hardcovers run $30-40. The subscription model is their most popular option: Chatbooks auto-creates a new book whenever you hit a certain number of new photos.

Best for: Parents who want an ongoing, low-effort way to turn phone photos into books without thinking about design.

The catch: Layouts are basic. There's no editorial curation — it pulls in your photos in order without judging quality. The result is more of a printed photo feed than a designed book. For a bigger comparison with Shutterfly, see Remeet vs Chatbooks.

4. Snapfish — Shutterfly's budget twin

Snapfish and Shutterfly are remarkably similar. Both offer template-based editors, similar product ranges, and aggressive sales. The key difference: Snapfish is usually cheaper. Softcovers start around $8, and their 50%+ sales happen frequently. An 80-page hardcover typically lands between $40-70.

The editor is functional but not inspiring — similar to Shutterfly but with fewer template options. Print quality is decent for the price. If you're leaving Shutterfly purely because of cost, Snapfish gives you essentially the same experience for less money.

Best for: Budget-conscious families who are comfortable with a template editor and just want a lower price than Shutterfly.

The catch: Same design limitations as Shutterfly. Same manual effort. Same common photo book mistakes if you're not careful with cropping and layout. Just cheaper. See Remeet vs Snapfish if you're weighing price against effort.

5. Google Photos — Already on your phone

Google Photos has a built-in photo book feature that most people don't know about. It can auto-suggest books based on trips, events, or date ranges. The AI picks photos, proposes a layout, and you review and edit from there.

It's surprisingly capable for a free app. The interface is clean, the AI suggestions are often decent, and since your photos are already there, there's no uploading step. Softcovers start around $15. Hardcovers run $20-40 depending on page count.

The limitations are real, though. Layout options are minimal. You can't do full-bleed pages or complex multi-photo spreads. There are no captions or chapter titles. And if you have thousands of photos, the AI's selection still needs significant manual curation.

Best for: People who want a simple book from a single trip or event and already use Google Photos. For a walkthrough, see our Google Photos to photo book guide.

The catch: Very limited design control. The books look clean but generic. Not ideal for large projects like a full-year family book or a wedding album. Compare AI photo book makers if you want smarter automation.

6. Apple Photos — Clean but Mac-only

Apple Photos offers a built-in book feature on Mac. The designs are clean and minimal — very Apple. The editor is straightforward, and print quality is good for the price. Softcovers start around $15, with hardcovers in the $30-50 range.

The biggest limitation is the platform: it only works on Mac. There's no web version, no Windows support, and no mobile editor. If you're already in the Apple ecosystem with iCloud Photos, it's a smooth experience. If not, it's not an option at all.

Best for: Mac users who want a clean, simple book without leaving the Apple ecosystem.

The catch: Mac only. Limited layout options. No captions or chapter structure. Fewer design choices than Mixbook or even Shutterfly.

7. Remeet — Done for you, zero design work

Remeet takes a completely different approach. Instead of giving you an editor and templates, it asks for one thing: a link to your photos. You share a Google Photos album, an iCloud link, or a Drive folder. Then a team of designers builds the book for you.

The process includes everything most people struggle with: photo selection, duplicate removal, cropping, chronological sorting, chapter structure, captions, layout design, and a page-by-page quality check. You receive a designed proof in 72 hours. Review it, request changes, and approve when you're happy.

Digital books start at $29 for 30 pages and $79 for 100 pages. Printed hardcovers start at $25. The sticker price is higher than Shutterfly's template-based books. But the total cost is different when you factor in time. A Shutterfly book typically takes 3-5 hours to design. At $30/hour, that's $90-150 of your time on top of the book price.

Best for: People who have thousands of photos, want a beautiful book, and don't have time (or interest) to design one themselves. See our done-for-you photo book service page for the full process.

The catch: You give up control. The designers make the initial choices — which photos, which layout, which captions. You can change anything, but the starting point is theirs, not yours. If you enjoy the design process, this takes that away.

When Shutterfly is still the right choice

To be fair, Shutterfly does some things that none of these alternatives match.

  • Same-day pickup. If you need a photo book printed and in your hands today, Shutterfly's partnership with retail locations makes this possible. No other service on this list can do that.
  • Product range. Calendars, mugs, blankets, ornaments, cards, prints — Shutterfly is a one-stop shop. If you're ordering a photo book plus holiday cards plus a mug, consolidating at Shutterfly makes sense.
  • Volume orders. Need 20 copies of the same book for a school event or family reunion? Shutterfly's bulk pricing is hard to beat.
  • Frequent deep sales. 40-50% off happens almost weekly. If you're patient and price-sensitive, Shutterfly on sale can be very affordable.

The abandonment factor

Here's something the comparison charts never show: most DIY photo book projects never get finished. People start with good intentions, spend an hour uploading photos and fighting the editor, realize they're only on page 8 of 60, and close the tab. The project sits in their cart for months, then expires.

This is true for Shutterfly. It's true for Mixbook. It's true for any service where you do the design work yourself. The more ambitious the project (a full year of family photos, a baby's first year, a big trip), the higher the dropout rate.

The cheapest photo book is the one that actually gets made. If you've tried Shutterfly and abandoned a project halfway through, the issue might not be the service — it might be the DIY approach itself.

The bottom line

Shutterfly is fine for simple projects with a few dozen photos. But for bigger books — a year of family memories, a baby's first year, a wedding — you deserve better tools, better design, or less work.

  • Better materials: Artifact Uprising
