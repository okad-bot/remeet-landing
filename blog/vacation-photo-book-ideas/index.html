Vacation Photo Book: Turn Any Trip Into a Keepsake

September 2026 · 10 min read

You came home from vacation with 800 photos on your phone. You posted a few to Instagram, texted a handful to your family, and the rest disappeared into your camera roll. Six months later, the trip feels like a blur.

A vacation photo book fixes that. It turns a scattered collection of images into a story you can hold, flip through, and relive whenever you want. But the gap between "I should make a book from that trip" and actually finishing one is wide. This guide closes it — with practical advice on selecting photos, organizing layouts, and making a book that captures how the trip actually felt.

Start by culling ruthlessly

The biggest mistake people make with vacation photo books is trying to include everything. You took 800 photos, but your book needs 60 to 100. That means letting go of roughly 90% of what you shot.

Here's a culling process that works:

  1. Delete the obvious rejects first. Blurry shots, accidental pocket photos, duplicates where you took the same scene five times. This usually cuts your collection by 30-40% with zero emotional cost.
  2. Pick one per moment. You have twelve photos of that sunset. Choose the best one. You have eight selfies at the same landmark. Keep one, maybe two. This is where the real reduction happens.
  3. Check for variety. Look at your remaining photos as a set. Do you have a mix of landscapes, food, people, details, and candid moments? If you have twenty landscape shots and three photos with people in them, rebalance. The best travel books alternate between the place and the people experiencing it.
  4. Set a target number. For a standard photo book, aim for 60-100 images. That gives you 30-50 pages with a comfortable mix of full-page photos and multi-image spreads.

If your photos are scattered across your phone, a partner's phone, and a camera, start by getting everything into one place. Exporting from Google Photos or organizing your phone photos first makes the culling process much easier.

Three ways to organize your trip

Once you have your final selection, you need a structure. There's no single right answer, but these three approaches cover most trip types.

Chronological (day by day)

The most intuitive approach. Start with Day 1 and move forward. This works especially well for road trips, multi-city tours, and any trip where the itinerary tells a natural story. Each day becomes a mini-chapter.

The risk is monotony. If every day looks the same — breakfast, sightseeing, dinner — the book can feel repetitive. Break it up by varying your page layouts and including detail shots between the big moments.

By location

If you visited multiple places, organize by destination rather than date. A two-week trip through Italy might have chapters for Rome, Florence, the Amalfi Coast, and Venice. Each section opens with a wide establishing shot of the place and closes with a detail that captures its character.

This approach works well when you spent multiple days in each location and the places themselves felt distinct.

By theme

Group photos by subject rather than time or place: all the food on one spread, all the architecture on another, all the candid people-watching moments together. This creates a more editorial, magazine-style feel.

Thematic organization works best for shorter trips or trips where the destination was less important than the experience — a weekend at a festival, a camping trip, a cruise.

What to capture beyond the landmarks

The photos that make a travel book feel alive aren't usually the postcard shots. Those are fine to include — you need at least a few establishing shots — but the book gets interesting when you go beyond them.

Here are the categories of photos that make travel books memorable:

  • Food and drink. What you ate, where you ate it, the table setup, the menu in a language you couldn't read. Food is one of the strongest memory triggers.
  • Street details. Door handles, tile patterns, hand-painted signs, a cat sleeping on a windowsill. These are the things you noticed and loved but might forget.
  • Transit moments. The view from the train window, waiting at the airport, loading the car, the rental bike parked outside a cafe. Travel is as much about the getting-there as the being-there.
  • People being people. Your travel companion reading a map, laughing at dinner, asleep on the plane. The candid, unposed moments that show who you were on this trip.
  • The unexpected. The wrong turn that led somewhere beautiful. The rainstorm that changed your plans. The street performer you watched for twenty minutes. The best trips are defined by what you didn't plan.

If you're reading this before your trip, make a mental note to photograph these things intentionally. If you're reading it after, you probably have more of them than you think — scroll past the landmark photos and look for the in-between moments.

Including memorabilia

Physical souvenirs don't fit in a photo book, but photos of them do. Scan or photograph these items and work them into your layouts:

  • Maps. A photo of the map you used, with your route highlighted, makes a great opening or closing page. Annotated maps showing where you stayed, ate, and explored add context that photos alone can't provide.
  • Boarding passes and tickets. Lay them flat, photograph them, and use the images as small accent elements alongside your photos. They anchor the trip in specific dates and places.
  • Postcards. If you bought postcards (or even better, wrote them), scan them. A postcard image paired with the actual photo you took at the same spot creates a nice visual contrast.
  • Receipts and menus. That handwritten dinner bill from the tiny restaurant in Lisbon. The cocktail menu from the rooftop bar. These details feel mundane in the moment but become evocative years later.
  • Journal entries. If you kept any notes during the trip — even just a few lines in your phone's Notes app — photograph or screenshot them. Handwritten notes have particular charm.

Layout ideas by trip type

Different kinds of trips call for different design approaches. Here's how to adjust your layout based on what kind of vacation you took. For more general layout advice, see our photo book layout tips.

Beach vacation

Beach trips produce lots of similar-looking photos — water, sand, sun. Combat the sameness by mixing in close-up detail shots (shells, footprints, drinks, sunscreen on kids' noses) between the wide ocean views. Use a warm, sandy color palette for any text or backgrounds. Keep the layout open and airy with plenty of white space.

Road trip

Road trips are inherently sequential, so a day-by-day structure works naturally. Include photos through the windshield, at gas stations, of roadside signs, and of the car itself. A map spread showing your route makes a great opening page. Consider adding distance or driving time as captions: "Mile 847. Wyoming."

European tour or multi-city trip

Use chapter dividers for each city or region. Open each chapter with a full-page establishing shot — a skyline, a famous landmark, or a street scene that captures the atmosphere. Within each chapter, mix architecture, food, and people. Include a small photo of your transportation between cities (trains are particularly photogenic).

Camping or hiking trip

Nature photography shines in larger formats, so use full-page and full-spread layouts generously. Mix wide landscape shots with camp-life details: the tent at dawn, cooking over a fire, muddy boots, trail markers. If you tracked your route with GPS, include a map of the trail. These books benefit from minimal text — let the wilderness speak for itself.

City break or weekend getaway

Short trips produce fewer photos, which actually makes design easier. A city break book might be only 20-30 pages, and that's perfect. Focus on the highlights: the best meal, the best view, the moment that defined the trip. Smaller books feel intimate and intentional rather than incomplete.

How many photos per trip?

A good rule of thumb: plan for about 15-20 photos per day of travel. A weeklong trip produces a book of 80-120 images across 40-60 pages. A long weekend needs just 40-60 photos. You can always add more pages, but starting with a target keeps the project manageable.

Writing captions that transport you back

Captions in a travel book should capture what photos can't: temperature, sound, smell, taste, the story behind the moment. A few approaches:

  • The sensory detail. "The market smelled like cumin and fresh bread. We bought too much of both."
  • The location note. "Vernazza, Cinque Terre. We climbed 400 steps to get to this view and it was worth every one."
  • The honest confession. "We were completely lost. This was the wrong bus. It turned out to be the best afternoon of the trip."
  • The simple fact. "Tuesday. Rain all day. The museum was empty and we had the Vermeer room to ourselves."

Don't caption every photo. Some images — especially landscapes and candid moments — are stronger without words. Use captions selectively, on the photos where the story behind the image adds something the viewer couldn't guess.

One book per trip or an annual compilation?

Both approaches work, and the right choice depends on how you travel.

One book per trip makes sense for big, significant vacations — a honeymoon, a family trip to Japan, a month in South America. These trips deserve their own dedicated book with enough pages to tell the full story. A travel photo book for a single major trip typically runs 60-80 pages.

An annual travel compilation works better if you take several shorter trips throughout the year — a few long weekends, a beach week, a holiday visit to family. Combining them into a single "Year of Travel" book keeps your shelf manageable and creates an interesting contrast between destinations.

If you're not sure, start with the trip you remember most vividly. Make that book first. You'll learn what you like and develop preferences that make the next one faster.

Don't let it wait

The sooner you make a travel book after the trip, the better. You'll remember details that help with captions, you'll recall which photos have stories behind them, and the emotional connection to the images is still fresh. If you wait a year, the project feels larger and the motivation smaller. A good target is starting the book within a month of returning home.

Design tips for travel books

A few design principles that apply specifically to vacation photo books:

  • Use the destination's color palette. Santorini books look great with white and blue accents. A fall foliage trip in New England calls for warm oranges and reds. Let the place influence the design, not just the photos.
  • Give landscapes room to breathe. Your best landscape photo deserves a full page or a full spread. Shrinking a panoramic sunset to share space with three other photos diminishes its impact.
  • Create rhythm with page variety. Alternate between full-page images, two-photo spreads, and grid layouts with four to six smaller images. This variation keeps the reader engaged across 40+ pages.
  • Open strong, close quiet. Start the book with an arrival moment — the airplane window, the first glimpse of the skyline, stepping off the boat. End with a departure shot or a quiet detail that captures the mood of the last day.
